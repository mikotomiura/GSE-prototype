# Outline — per-person learning in sequence models of keystroke logs

Status: outline of claims, evidence and placement, revised 2026-11-11. Target: User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction (empirical research paper). Numbers are medians over writers from four sequentially designed analyses and five later checks, each later check with its conditions committed before its code (3.4). Panels: CoAuthor 15-session panel (17 writers), CoAuthor 30-session panel (9 writers), CS1 five-assignment panel (23 writers); all-writer panels in S4. Model labels: hmm3 and hmm10 are HMMs with 3 and 10 states; ar1, ar2 and ar3 are AR(1)-HMMs with 1 to 3 states (ar1 is a first-order Markov chain). The shrinkage grid is in S8.

## Take-home

In an exploratory analysis of keystroke sequences symbolized over overlapping 30-second windows, a per-person sequence model gains a lot over a shared model of the same weak structure, but once structure and shrinkage are chosen on other people, what remains in the 15-session panel is below what the protocol can detect and does not beat the shared model's prior mode, which uses none of the person's data. We suggest that studies deciding for a whole panel compare per-person sequence models with the person's first-order Markov chain, with a shared model whose structure and shrinkage are chosen on other people, and with that model's prior mode.

## Contributions

C1. An evaluation protocol for per-person gains in sequence models of keystroke behaviour, and what it changes in one case: the gain that a weak comparison shows disappears against a stronger one.
- Protocol: on later data from the same person, compare with three models (a shared model of the same structure fitted without the target writer, its prior mode, and the person's own first-order Markov chain), and choose structure and shrinkage strength on other people, with the shared model itself among the candidates (3.3).
- Instance (CoAuthor, original symbolization): with hmm3 fixed, the per-person fit gains +0.188 nats per symbol at k = 5 (15 of 17 writers) over the shared hmm3 (4.1). Under the protocol ar3 is chosen in every cell, the remainder over the shared model is +4.1e-4 to +7.2e-4 in the 15-session panel, below the smallest difference the protocol detected in synthetic writers of that record length (4.3), and the per-person model does not beat the prior mode for k up to 15 (4.4). In the 30-session panel at k = 30 it does (4.4); with 9 writers we do not read this as a crossover.
- CS1: the hmm3 per-person fit does not consistently beat the shared model, and under the protocol the shared model itself is chosen for every writer, so CS1 cannot show a per-person gain (4.3).
- Compared with: the principle that selection must not use the evaluation data (Cawley and Talbot 2010; Varma and Simon 2006) and assessment of an individual by fitting on the other n − 1 people (Liu and Meng 2016, who note the cost in relevance to the individual) are established. Choosing interpolation weights on other users and testing on the same users' later text is the practice in personalized keyboard language models (Fowler et al. 2015; Shao et al. 2020; Adhikary and Vertanen 2023) and is not claimed as new. The per-person fit is maximum a posteriori adaptation toward a shared model, as in speaker adaptation (Gauvain and Lee 1994; Reynolds et al. 2000), not a multilevel HMM (Altman 2007; Aarts and Haslbeck 2025). Choosing the shared model itself (full pooling) on other people is a predictive choice among fixed candidates, not a boundary estimate of a variance component (Morris and Lysy 2012; Chung et al. 2013).
- Novelty (as found in our search, not established as absent): that weak comparisons make personalization look better is known for mood and stress estimation (DeMasi et al. 2017; LiKamWa et al. 2013), and learner-dependent HMMs have been fitted to keystroke features over 30-second windows (Uto et al. 2020). What we add is this comparison for per-person sequence models of keystroke behaviour, with the person's chain and the shared model's prior mode as comparison models. The search record is in S1.
- Strength: exploratory and on one dataset for the positive instance (Section 6).

Supporting evidence (not a separate contribution): the person's own first-order Markov chain is a strong baseline. On overlapping-window symbolizations the per-person three-state HMM loses to it (about −0.5 to −0.6 nats per symbol in CoAuthor and −1.1 in CS1), and ar1 shrunk toward the shared chain beats it by +0.043 at k = 5 (15-session panel, 17 of 17) (4.2).
- Compared with: within-data comparisons of an HMM and a first-order Markov chain by DIC (Shirley et al. 2010); per-person keystroke-timing HMMs improved by conditioning on the key sequence (Monaco and Tappert 2018); autoregressive HMMs repairing within-state autocorrelation (Stoye et al. 2025); first-order chains adequate for web navigation at the page level but not at the topical level (Singer et al. 2014); information criteria choosing too many states (Pohle et al. 2017). What we add is the comparison on later data from the same person.
- Small per-person gains have been reported before (a person-mean baseline that beat every general model for 89% of participants, although for 80% at least one personal model beat it, Aalbers et al. 2023; individualized knowledge tracing, Yudelson et al. 2013; Khajah et al. 2016); large gains have been reported against a single general model or with splits that mix a person's days across training and test (Weiss and Lockhart 2012; Taylor et al. 2020; Meegahapola et al. 2022); record-wise splits can inflate accuracy, and which split fits depends on the use case (Saeb et al. 2017; Little et al. 2017). Comparisons with a population baseline rather than the person's own average make mood models look better than they are (DeMasi et al. 2017). When per-person models overtake depends on the domain and the amount of data: a personal mood model overtook a hybrid after about 30 days (LiKamWa et al. 2013), and pooled models beat person-specific ones in experience-sampling data (Leenaerts et al. 2024).

## Comparison models and use case (defined once, in 3.3)

- Use case: predicting a new person's later sessions with a shared model fitted before that person arrived. The shared model is refitted without the target writer (leave one writer out). This simulates the use case for the fit and the evaluation, not for the choice: the choice uses other writers' results, whose shared models include the target writer's training units (Figure 1).
- Shared model: the same structure fitted by MAP on all other writers, excluding every writer's evaluation units, with a weak base prior (fixed pseudo-counts in every cell).
- Shared model's prior mode: the shared model's expected counts per symbol, normalized, without the base pseudo-counts. It uses none of the person's data and can give zero probability to a symbol pair the other writers never produced.
- Per-person fit: MAP estimate whose prior pseudo-counts are the base pseudo-counts plus tau times the shared expected counts. As tau grows the fit approaches the prior mode, not the shared model; tau = infinity in the grid denotes the shared model itself, a separate candidate. The sequence models have no person-level intercept.
- Person's first-order Markov chain: transition counts from the person's training symbols with +0.5 added to every cell.
- Choice on other people: the pair of structure and tau with the best median, over the other writers of the same panel and k, of their own per-person minus chain difference on their evaluation units; ties go to the larger tau and then the structure with fewer free parameters. The target writer's evaluation units enter no fit and no choice. This objective is not the target writer's predictive accuracy; a choice by the mean of other writers' held-out log-likelihood is reported as a check (4.4, S9).
- Main-table rows: the joint choice (main row), structure fixed at hmm3 and at ar3 (structure-only contrast), and ar1 against the person's chain (shrinkage-only contrast), each choosing tau from the grid and the shared model itself. Columns: per-person minus shared, minus prior mode, minus chain, and prior mode minus shared.

## Limitations (Section 6, stated once)

- Two datasets and one quantization for the main results, on overlapping-window symbolizations; the size of what remains depends on the symbolization (4.3). Sessions and assignments are not days. Results are not extended to other languages, input methods or populations.
- In CS1 about 72% of evaluation symbols are seconds with no key press in the last 30 s (about 5% in CoAuthor), so the CS1 measure is mostly the prediction of pause seconds; the CS1 evaluation unit is also a different assignment from the training units.
- The hidden states are not given names and are not read as cognitive or affective states; better prediction of the 26-symbol sequence is short-range sequential dependence, not structure of mental state.
- No significance tests are used and no threshold defines a meaningful difference; the structure-compensation check (S14) and the synthetic check (S15) use descriptive rules fixed before their computation. Comparisons of medians are not per-writer decompositions.
- The analyses were designed one after another, after seeing earlier results (3.4, S12). The structure grid stops at three states and ar3, its largest structure, was chosen in every cell on the original symbolization. Smoothed higher-order Markov chains and multilevel HMMs were not fitted.
- The protocol returns only part of a planted per-person difference and did not detect differences below 1e-2 (k = 15) or 3e-2 (k = 5) at the CoAuthor record length; differences planted in the symbol-to-symbol transitions are the only kind checked (S15). A remainder below that limit, or a per-person model that does not beat the prior mode, does not show that per-person differences are absent. The synthetic writers did not reproduce the real ordering of prior mode and shared model.
- At large shrinkage, a finite tau of 10^5 or more cannot be separated from the shared model or its prior mode as a per-person gain. The prior-mode comparison was made on the original symbolization only. The 30-session panel has 9 writers, and at its largest k the per-person model beats the prior mode; longer records may change the picture.
- The choice makes one decision for a whole panel: a difference planted in 5 of 17 synthetic writers was not detected in the panel median (S15). The choice is not fixed before the target writer arrives, and a nested design that removes the target writer from the other writers' shared models was not run (3.3).
- The prior mode can give zero probability to a held-out symbol pair (one or two writers per cell in CoAuthor); it is used as a comparison model, not as a candidate (4.4).
- The +0.5 added to every cell of the person's chain is fixed. We did not compare with a shared model that includes the target writer's training units.
- The unsmoothed-symbol check removes the smoothing and, in one variant, the window overlap, but the seconds since the last key press still carry memory across windows (S13).
