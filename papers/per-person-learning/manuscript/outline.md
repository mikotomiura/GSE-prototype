# Outline — per-person learning in sequence models of keystroke logs

Status: outline of claims, evidence and placement, revised 2026-11-11. Target: User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction (empirical research paper). Numbers are medians over writers from four sequentially designed analyses and three later checks (unsmoothed symbols with a randomized-quantile pseudo-residual check, Dunn and Smyth 1996; a check of the structure-compensation reading; and the shared model's prior mode placed among the comparison models and the candidates, recomputed from stored per-writer values without new fits). A synthetic check of the protocol's sensitivity, with its conditions fixed before its code, is reported in 4.3 and S15. Panels: CoAuthor 15-session panel (17 writers), CoAuthor 30-session panel (9 writers), CS1 five-assignment panel (23 writers); the all-writer panels are defined in S4. Model labels: hmm3 and hmm10 are HMMs with 3 and 10 states; ar1, ar2 and ar3 are AR(1)-HMMs with 1 to 3 states (ar1 is a first-order Markov chain). The shrinkage grid is tau in {0, 100, 1,000, 10^4, 3 x 10^4, 10^5, 3 x 10^5, 10^6, infinity}; earlier analyses on a grid up to 10^4 are marked.

## Take-home

In an exploratory analysis of keystroke sequences symbolized over overlapping 30-second windows, whether a per-person sequence model beats a shared model depends on what it is compared with: against a shared model of the same weak structure it can gain a lot, but once the structure and the amount of shrinkage are chosen on other people, the protocol returns at most about 1e-3 nat per symbol over the shared model, and the chosen per-person model does not beat the shared model's prior mode, which uses none of the person's data. We suggest that studies that decide for a whole panel whether to use per-person sequence models of keystroke behaviour compare them with the person's own first-order Markov chain, with a shared model whose structure and shrinkage are chosen on other people, and with that shared model's prior mode, placed both as a comparison model and as a candidate; how much remains depends on the symbolization.

## Contributions

C1. An evaluation protocol for per-person gains in sequence models of keystroke behaviour, and what it changes in one case.
- Protocol: on later data from the same person, place three comparison models (a shared model of the same structure, fitted without the target writer; its prior mode; and the person's own first-order Markov chain), and choose the structure and the shrinkage strength on other people, with the shared model itself (no per-person deviation, tau = infinity) and its prior mode among the candidates so that a choice at the edge of the grid can be read.
- Instance (CoAuthor, original symbolization): with hmm3 fixed (tau chosen on other people: 0, no shrinkage, for every writer at k = 5 and 15), the per-person fit gains +0.188 nats per symbol at k = 5 training sessions (IQR +0.103 to +0.321; 15 of 17 writers favour it) and +0.165 at k = 15 (17 of 17) over the shared hmm3. Under the protocol ar3, the largest structure in the grid, is chosen in every cell, so the structure choice sits at the edge of the grid; in the 15-session panel the per-person minus shared difference is +4.1e-4 to +7.2e-4 for k = 1 to 15 (positive for 16 or 17 of 17 writers). With the prior mode among the comparison models and the candidates, the mode is chosen for at most 1 of 17 writers (at k = 1 to 8; that writer's held-out sequence has zero probability under its prior mode) and this row barely changes (only k = 5 moves, from +5.5e-4 to +5.3e-4), but the chosen per-person model does not beat the prior mode: per-person minus mode is −3.5e-4 to −1.6e-4 (positive for 2 to 6 of 17 writers) for k = 1 to 15. The 1e-3 is the amount the protocol returns, not a bound on per-person differences: in synthetic writers it returned about 0.1 to 0.4 of a planted difference and did not detect 1e-3 or 3e-3 at k = 15 (4.3, S15). The remainder is not read as a per-person gain because the per-person model does not beat the prior mode; in addition, the gap between a finite tau of 10^5 or more and the shared model itself cannot be separated from a per-person gain. In the 30-session panel at k = 30, where 3 x 10^4 is chosen for 8 of 9 writers, the difference is +1.1e-3 (6 of 9 positive) and per-person minus mode is +5.4e-4 (7 of 9 positive); both are reported as values only; so are the ar1 values with the structure fixed (+0.003 and +0.005 at k = 20 and 30).
- CS1: the hmm3 per-person fit does not consistently beat the shared model (medians 0 to +0.028 across k, at most 61% of writers favouring it), and under the protocol the prior mode is chosen for every writer (without it among the candidates, the shared model itself, tau = infinity), so per-person minus mode is zero by construction, not an observation; per-person minus shared (+2.0e-4) is then the gap between the mode and the shared model, not a per-person gain.
- Compared with: the principle that selection must not use the evaluation data (Cawley and Talbot 2010; Varma and Simon 2006) and assessment of an individual by fitting on the other n − 1 people (Liu and Meng 2016, who note the cost in relevance to the individual) are established. Choosing interpolation weights on other users and testing on the same users' later text is the practice in personalized keyboard language models (Fowler et al. 2015; Shao et al. 2020; Adhikary and Vertanen 2023) and is not claimed as new. The per-person fit is maximum a posteriori adaptation toward a shared model, as in speaker adaptation (Gauvain and Lee 1994; Reynolds et al. 2000), not a multilevel HMM (Altman 2007; Aarts and Haslbeck 2025). Choosing tau = infinity (full pooling, which corresponds to a between-person variance of zero) on other people differs from a boundary estimate of a variance component (Morris and Lysy 2012; Chung et al. 2013): it is a predictive choice among fixed candidates.
- Novelty (as found in our search, not established as absent): applying this comparison to per-person sequence models of keystroke behaviour, and reporting that, when the comparison model's structure is improved, the visible per-person gain shrinks to a remainder that the shared model's prior mode, using none of the person's data, is not beaten by. The search record is in S1.
- Strength: observed in CoAuthor on the original symbolization (exploratory; the candidate families and the grid were fixed after seeing earlier results, see 3.4); CS1 cannot show a per-person gain under the protocol, because the prior mode (or, without it among the candidates, the shared model itself) is chosen for every writer. The instance depends on the symbolization (4.3): with unsmoothed symbols in overlapping windows the remainder is also below 1e-3, but with non-overlapping windows the protocol picks hmm3 in CoAuthor at k = 5 and 15 and the per-person difference stays positive. Generality of the protocol is argued, not tested.

Supporting evidence (not a separate contribution): the person's own first-order Markov chain is a strong baseline.
- On overlapping-window symbolizations the per-person three-state HMM loses to it (about −0.5 to −0.6 nats per symbol in CoAuthor and −1.1 in CS1, negative for all or all but one writer); with non-overlapping windows (direction only) it beats the chain in CoAuthor (S13). Shrinking the person's first-order chain toward the shared chain (ar1, tau chosen on other people, 10^6 for every writer, so close to the shared chain itself) beats it by +0.043 at k = 5 (15-session panel, 17 of 17). The margin of the shared model alone (tau = infinity) shrinks with k; for ar1 in the 30-session panel at k = 30 it falls below the person's chain.
- Compared with: within-data comparisons of an HMM and a first-order Markov chain by DIC (Shirley et al. 2010); per-person keystroke-timing HMMs improved by conditioning on the key sequence (Monaco and Tappert 2018); autoregressive HMMs repairing within-state autocorrelation (Stoye et al. 2025); first-order chains adequate for web navigation at the page level but not at the topical level (Singer et al. 2014); information criteria choosing too many states (Pohle et al. 2017). What we add is the comparison on later data from the same person.
- Small per-person gains have been reported before (a person-mean baseline that beat every general model for 89% of participants and that the median error of each personal model did not clearly improve on, although for 80% of participants at least one personal model beat it, Aalbers et al. 2023; individualized knowledge tracing, Yudelson et al. 2013; Khajah et al. 2016); large gains have been reported against a single general model or with splits that mix a person's days across training and test (Weiss and Lockhart 2012; Taylor et al. 2020; Meegahapola et al. 2022); record-wise splits can inflate accuracy (Saeb et al. 2017), and which split fits depends on the use case (Saeb et al. 2017; Little et al. 2017).

Supporting results moved to the supplement: the full decomposition of the remainder at very large shrinkage, reported as values only (S9), its check on synthetic writers (S15), and logistic models on a proxy label (S10).

## Comparison models and use case (defined once, in 3.3)

- Use case: predicting a new person's later sessions with a shared model that was fitted before that person arrived. Each target writer is treated as such a new person: the shared model is refitted without that writer (leave one writer out), which simulates this use case rather than testing one fixed shared model. The simulation covers the fit and the evaluation but not the choice: the choice of structure and tau uses other writers' results, whose shared models include the target writer's training units (Figure 1), so it is not a choice fixed before the target writer arrives.
- Shared model: the same structure fitted on all other writers in the dataset, excluding every writer's evaluation units (writers without evaluation units contribute all their units).
- Person's first-order Markov chain: transition counts from the person's training symbols with +0.5 added to every cell.
- Shared model's prior mode: the normalized expected counts of the shared model (fitted without the target writer), the centre toward which the per-person fit is shrunk; it uses none of the person's data and differs from the shared parameters by the smoothing of the prior.
- Per-person fit: MAP estimate shrunk toward the shared model with strength tau. The sequence models have no person-level intercept.
- Choice on other people: the pair of structure and tau is the one with the best median, over the other writers of the same panel and k, of their own per-person minus chain difference on their evaluation units, ties broken toward the larger tau and then the structure with fewer free parameters; the target writer's values do not enter the choice. The objective is this median of differences; it is neither the target writer's predictive accuracy nor the per-person gain over the shared model, and a median of differences does not preserve the order of the values themselves, so the joint choice and the choice with structure fixed can pick different candidates for the same writers (S9). The target writer's evaluation units enter no fit and no choice; its training units do enter the other writers' shared models, as any other writer's do. Figure 1 shows which units enter each fit and each choice.
- Adjusted contrasts (not effects of one factor alone): structure (hmm3 against ar3, each with its own tau chosen on other people) and shrinkage (ar1 with tau chosen on other people against the person's chain, the same first-order structure). Both are rows of the main table; columns are named per-person minus shared and per-person minus chain throughout.

## Limitations (Section 6, stated once)

- Two datasets and one quantization for the main results, on overlapping-window symbolizations (4.3). Sessions and assignments are not days. Results are not extended to other languages, input methods, or other populations of writers or programmers.
- The hidden states are not given names and are not read as cognitive or affective states; better prediction of the 26-symbol sequence is short-range sequential dependence, not structure of mental state.
- No significance tests are used, and no threshold defines a meaningful difference; the structure-compensation check uses descriptive reading rules with cut points fixed before its computation (S14), and the synthetic sensitivity check uses a detection rule fixed before its computation (S15). Comparisons of medians are not per-writer decompositions. "At most about 1e-3" describes the values the protocol returned, not a threshold or a bound.
- The analyses were designed one after another, after seeing earlier results (3.4, S12). The structure grid stops at three states and was not extended in this study; on the original symbolization ar3 was chosen in every cell, at the upper end. Smoothed higher-order Markov chains and multilevel HMMs that estimate the between-person variance were not fitted.
- At large shrinkage, the difference between a finite tau of 10^5 or more and the shared model itself cannot be separated from a per-person gain; the remainder is not read as one, because the per-person model does not beat the shared model's prior mode in the 15-session panel (4.3). That check and the decomposition of the remainder (S9) were made only on the original symbolization, and the decomposition did not separate a planted difference from none in synthetic writers (S15), so it is not used as evidence. The 30-session panel has 9 writers, and its largest k shows interior shrinkage and larger values for ar1; longer records may change the picture.
- In synthetic writers matched in record length to the CoAuthor 15-session panel, the protocol did not detect planted per-person differences in symbol-to-symbol transitions of 1e-3 and 3e-3 nat per symbol at k = 15 (nor any level up to 1e-2 at k = 5) (S15). The small remainder therefore does not show that smaller per-person differences are absent. The check plants one kind of difference only (in the symbol-to-symbol transitions within states; the hidden-state dynamics are shared).
- The choice on other people makes one decision for a whole panel. In synthetic writers with a difference planted in 5 of 17 writers, the panel median did not detect it, although those writers' per-person minus shared was positive at k = 15 (S15); differences confined to a few writers are not detected by this protocol.
- The prior mode can give zero probability to a held-out symbol pair; in the CoAuthor panels this happened for one or two writers per cell, and at k = 1 to 8 the one writer to whom the mode was assigned was such a writer (4.3).
- The +0.5 added to every cell of the person's first-order chain is fixed, not chosen on other people.
- We did not compare with a shared model that includes the target writer's training units (use case in 3.3).
- The unsmoothed-symbol check removes the smoothing and, in its second variant, the window overlap, but not every source of dependence: the seconds since the last key press still carry memory across windows. Details and the pseudo-residual caveats are in S13; the structure-compensation caveats are in S14.
