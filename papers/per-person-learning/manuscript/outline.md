# Outline — per-person learning in sequence models of keystroke logs

Status: outline of claims, evidence and placement, revised 2026-11-10. Target: User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction (empirical research paper). Numbers are medians over writers from four sequentially designed analyses and two later checks (unsmoothed symbols with a randomized-quantile pseudo-residual check, Dunn and Smyth 1996; and a check of the structure-compensation reading). Panels: CoAuthor 15-session panel (17 writers), CoAuthor 30-session panel (9 writers), CS1 five-assignment panel (23 writers); the all-writer panels are defined in S4. Model labels: hmm3 and hmm10 are HMMs with 3 and 10 states; ar1, ar2 and ar3 are AR(1)-HMMs with 1 to 3 states (ar1 is a first-order Markov chain). The shrinkage grid is tau in {0, 100, 1,000, 10^4, 3 x 10^4, 10^5, 3 x 10^5, 10^6, infinity}; earlier analyses on a grid up to 10^4 are marked.

## Take-home

Whether a per-person sequence model of a writer's keystrokes beats a shared model depends on what it is compared with: against a shared model of the same weak structure it can gain a lot, but once the structure and the amount of shrinkage are chosen on other people, the per-person gain shrinks to a few ten-thousandths of a nat per symbol. For sequence models of keystroke behaviour, we suggest that studies of per-person user models compare them with a shared model whose structure has been improved, chosen without the person's evaluation data.

## Contributions

C1. An evaluation protocol for per-person gains in sequence models, and what it changes in one case.
- Protocol: on later data from the same person, place two comparison models (a shared model of the same structure, fitted without the target writer, and the person's own first-order Markov chain), and choose the structure and the shrinkage strength on other people, with the shared model itself (no per-person deviation, tau = infinity) among the candidates so that a choice at the edge of the grid can be read.
- Instance (CoAuthor): with hmm3 fixed, the per-person fit gains +0.188 nats per symbol at k = 5 training sessions (IQR +0.103 to +0.321; 15 of 17 writers favour it) and +0.165 at k = 15 (17 of 17) over the shared hmm3. Under the protocol ar3 is chosen in every cell, and the per-person minus shared difference is +4.1e-4 to +7.2e-4 for k = 1 to 15 in the 15-session panel (16 or 17 of 17 writers favour it) and up to +0.001 in the 30-session panel (k = 30, 6 of 9). This remainder is small but consistent in sign. Comparing medians, it is larger in the gap between the prior mode and the shared parameters than in the contribution of the person's own data, which is negative (S9). For reference, ar1 with the structure fixed rather than chosen gives +0.003 and +0.005 in the 30-session panel at k = 20 and 30, with an interior shrinkage strength chosen.
- CS1: the hmm3 per-person fit does not consistently beat the shared model (medians 0 to +0.028 across k, at most 61% of writers favouring it), and under the protocol the shared model itself (tau = infinity) is chosen for every writer, so the per-person difference is zero by construction, not an observation.
- Compared with: the principle that selection must not use the evaluation data (Cawley and Talbot 2010; Varma and Simon 2006) and assessment of an individual by fitting on the other n − 1 people (Liu and Meng 2016, who note the cost in relevance to the individual) are established. Choosing interpolation weights on other users and testing on the same users' later text is the practice in personalized keyboard language models (Fowler et al. 2015; Shao et al. 2020; Adhikary and Vertanen 2023) and is not claimed as new. The per-person fit is maximum a posteriori adaptation toward a shared model, as in speaker adaptation (Gauvain and Lee 1994; Reynolds et al. 2000), not a multilevel HMM (Altman 2007; Aarts and Haslbeck 2025). Choosing tau = infinity (full pooling, which corresponds to a between-person variance of zero) on other people differs from a boundary estimate of a variance component (Morris and Lysy 2012; Chung et al. 2013): it is a predictive choice among fixed candidates.
- Novelty (as found in our search, not established as absent): applying this comparison to per-person sequence models of behavioural symbol sequences, and reporting that the per-person gain disappears when the comparison model's structure is improved. The search record is in S1.
- Strength: observed in CoAuthor on the original symbolization (exploratory; the analyses were designed one after another, see 3.4); CS1 cannot show a per-person gain under the protocol, because the shared model itself is chosen. On unsmoothed symbols the CoAuthor hmm3 gain remains (+0.140 at k = 5, +0.155 at k = 15; S13). Small per-person gains have been reported before (a person-mean baseline that neither personal nor general models beat, Aalbers et al. 2023; individualized knowledge tracing, Yudelson et al. 2013; Khajah et al. 2016); large gains have been reported against a single general model or with splits that mix a person's days across training and test (Weiss and Lockhart 2012; Taylor et al. 2020; Meegahapola et al. 2022); record-wise splits can inflate accuracy (Saeb et al. 2017), although which split fits depends on the use case (Little et al. 2017). Generality of the protocol is argued, not tested.

Supporting evidence (not a separate contribution): the person's own first-order Markov chain is a strong baseline.
- The per-person three-state HMM loses to it (about −0.5 nats per symbol in CoAuthor and −1.1 in CS1, negative for all or all but one writer), while the protocol's choice (ar3) beats it for every writer at every k in all three panels (+0.220 at k = 1 to +0.020 at k = 15 in the 15-session panel; +0.050 to +0.029 in CS1). Shrinking the person's first-order chain toward the shared chain (ar1, tau chosen on other people) already gives +0.043 at k = 5, of the +0.059 of ar3 (15-session panel, 17 of 17 writers favour both). The margin of the shared model alone (tau = infinity) shrinks with k; for ar1 in the 30-session panel at k = 30 it falls below the person's chain.
- Compared with: within-data comparisons of an HMM and a first-order Markov chain by DIC (Shirley et al. 2010); per-person keystroke-timing HMMs improved by conditioning on the key sequence (Monaco and Tappert 2018); autoregressive HMMs repairing within-state autocorrelation (Stoye et al. 2025); first-order chains adequate for web navigation at the page level but not at the topical level (Singer et al. 2014); information criteria choosing too many states (Pohle et al. 2017). What we add is the comparison on later data from the same person.
- On unsmoothed symbols in overlapping windows both differences keep their direction and at least half their size (S13). The model check is summarized in 4.2 and the structure-compensation check in Section 5; neither is used to explain the instance.

Supporting results moved to the supplement: the full decomposition of the remainder at very large shrinkage (S9) and logistic models on a proxy label (S10).

## Comparison models (defined once, in 3.3)

- Shared model: the same structure fitted on all other writers in the dataset, excluding every writer's evaluation units (writers without evaluation units contribute all their units).
- Person's first-order Markov chain: transition counts from the person's training symbols with +0.5 added to every cell.
- Per-person fit: MAP estimate shrunk toward the shared model with strength tau. The sequence models have no person-level intercept.
- Two contrasts separate the parts: structure (hmm3 against ar3, each with its own tau chosen on other people by the same rule) and shrinkage (ar1 with tau chosen on other people against the person's chain, the same first-order structure). Both are rows of the main table.

## Limitations (Section 6, stated once)

- Two datasets and one quantization for the main results. Sessions and assignments are not days. Results are not extended to other languages, input methods, or other populations of writers or programmers.
- The hidden states are not given names and are not read as cognitive or affective states; better prediction of the 26-symbol sequence is short-range sequential dependence, not structure of mental state.
- No significance tests are used. The main results use no thresholds; the structure-compensation check uses descriptive reading rules with cut points fixed before its computation. Numbers are medians with interquartile ranges, the share of writers favouring the per-person fit and n; comparisons of medians are not per-writer decompositions.
- The analyses were designed one after another, after seeing earlier results (3.4, S12).
- The instance is a measurement on the original symbolization. The check on unsmoothed symbols removes the smoothing and, in its second variant, the window overlap, but not every source of dependence: the seconds since the last key press still carry memory across windows. The second variant has about 1/30 of the symbols, so only the direction of its differences is read; in it the protocol picks hmm3 rather than an AR(1)-HMM in CoAuthor, the hmm3 gain reverses in CS1 (−0.003), and the hmm3 disadvantage against the person's chain reverses in CoAuthor.
- The pseudo-residuals are read only as a check of the dependence a model leaves on the two symbol axes at lags 1 to 10, not as evidence that a model is correct. On the original and unsmoothed symbols in overlapping windows, the three-state AR(1)-HMM and the first-order Markov chain both leave 0.02 or less at lag 1, and which of the two leaves less differs by cell.
- The structure-compensation check is read only by its pre-set rules. Its permutation removes slow drift in symbol frequencies as well as first-order dependence, and its transfer part, which uses unshrunk fits, cannot separate person-specific structure from overfitting. Its 10-, 26- and 50-symbol quantizations are used only in that check.
