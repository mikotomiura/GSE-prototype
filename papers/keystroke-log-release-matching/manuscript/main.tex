\title[Matching processed keystroke logs to texts]{Hiding the Text, Keeping the Process: Matching Processed
Keystroke Logs to Texts Held by Others}

\author{Anonymous}
\affiliation{%
  \institution{Anonymous}
  \country{Anonymous}}

\renewcommand{\shortauthors}{Anonymous}

\begin{abstract}
  Keystroke logs record how a text was written. One public release of essay logs masked every letter and digit, with
  the stated goal of preventing reproduction of the essays, yet the essays had been collected and scored and remained
  with others. We ask how far each form of processing keeps a keystroke log apart from its text against a party that
  holds candidate texts, and what each form costs the process measures of writing research. In a preregistered
  evaluation on 500 writers sampled from a public corpus of 4,992 essays with their logs, attackers that know the processing chose the true
  text among $N$ candidates on the same prompt. Against the masking, an attacker that rebuilds the final draft chose
  the true essay among 1,000 for every log (100.0\%, 95\% interval 100.0 to 100.0), 98 percentage points above an
  attacker that uses only the total length. Keeping only the categories of the pressed keys and their press times still
  left 86.7\% (83.5 to 89.5). Every form that kept ten process measures close to their unprocessed values left the strongest attacker at
  70\% or more, and the forms that brought it near the total length kept the measures at a median correlation of at
  most 0.70 or not at all. On Japanese translation logs typed with an input method, matching without any string
  exceeded chance and the total length. The results describe the attackers tried and are not bounds on the risk.
\end{abstract}
