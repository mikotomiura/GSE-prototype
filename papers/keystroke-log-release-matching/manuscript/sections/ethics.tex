\begin{ethics}
\paragraph{Review by an ethics committee.}
No institutional ethics committee was available to the authors, and no external committee or board reviewed the
study. As a safeguard, before acquiring the data, we asked the authors of the KLiCKe corpus about this use, and the
preregistration states that the computation would be stopped, and the acquired data deleted, if they raised a concern;
they raised none (below). The study collects no new data. It uses two public corpora and a public
model under their terms, identifies no individual and reports only aggregates.

\paragraph{The data and the consent of their participants.}
The authors of the KLiCKe corpus informed us (personal communication) that its collection was
approved by the Institutional Review Board of Georgia State University; that the participants, recruited through
Amazon Mechanical Turk, gave informed consent to the use of their data for research purposes and were informed that
their data would be ``used for research purposes by the broader research community''; that the data were reviewed for
personally identifiable information before release; the terms of use are given in Section~\ref{sec:data:corpora}. The
published description of the corpus states that participants received an informed consent form outlining the study's
objectives and procedures~\cite{tian2025klicke}. We describe the scope of consent in the words of the corpus authors
and do not claim that participants consented to any particular form of public release. The corpus authors saw no
particular concern with this analysis and recommended reporting the findings in aggregate and not disclosing
potentially sensitive or identifying information about individual participants. The corpus authors read a draft
of the paper before submission, including the passages that report what they told us, and raised no objection to them. The paper that describes the collection of ENJA15 states that its participants ``signed a
form in which they agreed that their translation data would be made publicly available under a creative commons
license''~\cite{carl2016lrec}; we follow the more restrictive of the two licence statements of the database that
distributes it~\cite{crittlicense}. We did not acquire the demographic file of KLiCKe, whose questionnaire asked for
the participants' Mechanical Turk worker IDs~\cite{tian2025klicke}.

\paragraph{The competition data.}
The competition data, which hide the texts, were not used. We did not examine whether they share essays with KLiCKe,
although the authors of KLiCKe told us that the competition data were drawn from the KLiCKe corpus before it was
released (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model:settings}). The competition data are a release made by another party under its
own rules, with the stated goal of preventing the reproduction of the texts; we do not know its conditions beyond its
public pages or what its releasing party intended. Matching its logs, or comparing them with KLiCKe, would undo that
goal for a real release by our own act, and it is not needed to evaluate the processing.

\paragraph{Stakeholders and what they could lose.}
The stakeholders are the writers whose logs are in the two corpora, the writers of other released logs (including the
competition data, some of whose writers may also be in KLiCKe), the parties that release keystroke logs, and readers who could reuse the procedures described here.
What the writers could lose is the link between their writing process, their scores and their texts. If no one misuses
the procedures, the writers of the two corpora lose nothing new from them: their texts and logs are already public, no result is
published for an individual writer, and no text or log is excerpted. If someone reuses the procedures against a
release that hides texts, the writers of that release could have their logs linked to their texts. The procedures are
of known types (Section~\ref{sec:related}), so this risk does not arise from this paper alone; what the paper adds is
a measurement, on two corpora, of how much each kind of processing reduced matching and kept the process measures. By
naming the competition data next to a public corpus whose procedure names the same platform, the same kind of prompts
and the same sequence of tasks, the paper also points to a release against which the procedures could be tried; we
did not try them. Reporting where the competition data come from is
itself a disclosure. It tells readers that, for competition essays that are in the released KLiCKe corpus, the records
of the two releases can be put in correspondence without our procedures, which would link the competition records,
with any column found only there, to public texts. As far as we found, neither the paper that describes KLiCKe nor
the winning write-up states this~\cite{tian2025klicke,inubushi2024first}. We report it because a party that relies on
processing should know that a later release of the same texts undoes it, and because leaving out what we were told
would misstate the release on which the evaluation is modelled. We state it as we were told it, without making the
correspondence, and the authors of KLiCKe read this account before submission.

\paragraph{Reasons from duties.}
Apart from these consequences, the study keeps within the purpose to which the participants agreed (research use of
their data), collects no new data, does not acquire the demographic file, and does not use the competition data, a
release made by another party under its own rules (above).

\paragraph{Weighing harms and benefits.}
We also weighed the study against the principles of the Menlo Report~\cite{dittrich2012menlo}. \emph{Respect for
persons:} the participants agreed to the use of their data for research; this study evaluates how well the release of
such data protects them, collects nothing new, identifies no one and publishes no result for an individual writer (with the
one exception under the handling of the data) and no excerpt of a text or a log. \emph{Beneficence:} the harm is that the paper describes procedures that link processed
logs to texts; the benefit is that parties who release keystroke logs learn how much each processing step reduced
matching by the attackers tried here, and how much of the standard process measures it kept, before they release more
data. The attacks evaluated here are of known types (Section~\ref{sec:related}); what is new is their evaluation, on
public keystroke data whose texts are known, against the masking used in one public release and against alternatives.
\emph{Justice:} no new burden falls on participants, and the benefit reaches every writer whose keystroke logs are
released. \emph{Respect for law and public interest:} the data and the public KeyTAR model are used under their
licences and terms, their sources are cited, and the study and its analysis plan were preregistered before any data
were acquired. The code is published only for the two corpora evaluated here; no code that reads
other released logs, and in particular the competition data, is written or published (see the open science section).
This withholds a ready tool aimed at a particular release; since the columns of the two releases are close, it does
little to stop a party determined to adapt the code, and for competition essays that are in KLiCKe no such code is
needed (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model:settings}).

\paragraph{Handling of the data.}
The logs and texts of both corpora are public, but they are kept outside the study repository, on the authors'
computers. Only aggregates are put in the repository and reported. Results per writer and excerpts of texts and logs
are not published, with one exception: since KeyTAR is reported with and without one writer
(Appendix~\ref{app:checks:deviations}), the difference gives that writer's aggregate values for KeyTAR, and the writer can
be located in the sample drawn with the registered seed. We report both because the generation on the CPU could change
that writer's reconstructions, and the comparison shows that it did not change the results. These values can be recomputed by anyone from the public
data, the code we publish and the public model, so they add no information. Logs and texts are not sent to any external language model or other external service. KeyTAR runs
on a local GPU, and its reconstructions are stored next to the logs and are not read, printed or published.

\paragraph{Interests.}
The authors develop software that records keystroke logs for research and plan to release keystroke logs collected
with it; the results of this paper bear on how such logs should be processed before release. The authors have no
financial interest in the results, and the study received no funding.
\end{ethics}
