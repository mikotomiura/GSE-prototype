\paragraph{Matching and reconstruction from keystrokes.}
Matching a log to candidate texts is a known kind of attack, and we do not claim a stronger attack. Inter-keystroke
timings leaked by interactive SSH sessions were shown to speed up the search for typed passwords~\cite{song2001timing}.
Keystroke timings alone have been used to decide which of a set of candidate texts was typed~\cite{gonzalez2021reverse}. KeyTAR
reconstructs English input from inter-keystroke timings with a fine-tuned language model, without
candidates~\cite{qiu2026keytar}, and reports that reconstruction ``is extremely sensitive to the accuracy of retrieved
inter-keystroke timings''. Outside keystroke logs, the sequence of packet lengths of encrypted voice calls identifies
spoken phrases among candidates, and padding the lengths removes most of the effect~\cite{wright2008spotme}; the
timing of keystrokes and the positions of spaces identify search queries among candidates~\cite{monaco2019searching}.
Most of these works observe typing through a side channel, and the search-query attack assumes that only letters and
the space are typed~\cite{monaco2019searching}. Here the attacker holds a released log of writing, with its revisions,
and the processing is chosen by the party that releases it.

\paragraph{Processing of keystroke data.}
The processing steps evaluated here are not new. Substitution, permutation and suppression of keystrokes have been
proposed to protect what is typed in behavioural authentication on smartphones, and were evaluated by the error rates
of authentication~\cite{vassallo2017privacy}. Rounding the latencies in typing profiles reduced the identification of
students~\cite{leinonen2017preventing}. A survey of anonymisation for behavioural biometric data lists such
techniques, against identification and for protecting what is typed, and none of those it lists for keystrokes is
evaluated by matching to texts; it notes that ``most anonymization techniques are only evaluated rudimentarily with
the assumption of a weak attacker''~\cite{hanisch2021survey}. Our forms are our own definitions after these names.

\paragraph{Auxiliary information and uniqueness.}
Records in a release can be linked through auxiliary information that the releasing party did not
anticipate~\cite{narayanan2008robust}. The share of records that a few known points single out decreases only slowly
as a dataset grows~\cite{farzanehfar2021risk}, and whether uniqueness in a sample indicates the likelihood of a
correct re-identification in the population is disputed~\cite{sanchez2016comment,rocher2019estimating}. A university
that holds data about students decides what to release, to whom and for what purpose~\cite{borgman2018open}. For
processed logs of writing, the auxiliary information is the text (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model:auxiliary}).

\paragraph{Releases of keystroke logs of writing and programming.}
Earlier work on releasing keystroke data considered an attacker with a labelled sample of the same person's keystrokes
and the goal of identification (Section~\ref{sec:threat-model:auxiliary}); it does not evaluate matching to texts, and
we do not claim that no work considers texts. Public corpora of writing processes with their texts, such as KLiCKe~\cite{tian2025klicke}
and ENJA15~\cite{carl2016lrec}, make such an evaluation possible, because the true text of every log is known.

\paragraph{What is new.}
What is new is the evaluation, on public keystroke data whose texts are known, of the masking used in one public release and
of alternative processing, by attacks that match logs to texts, together with what each form costs the process measures
of writing research. In the searches described in the open science section, we found no such evaluation.
The searches behind this statement are described in the open science section.
