\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

% What this part is meant to establish and what is measured. What was found and why it matters are written in
% Section 7 after the results.
The evaluation is meant to show, for each form, how far above chance and above the total length the attackers choose
the true text among $N$ candidates, and how far the process measures can still be computed. This section states how
candidate sets are built (\S\ref{sec:protocol:candidates}), what is reported for matching
(\S\ref{sec:protocol:quantities}) and for the process measures (\S\ref{sec:protocol:utility}), how the two are
summarised (\S\ref{sec:protocol:summary}), the diagnostics (\S\ref{sec:protocol:diagnostics}), the Japanese translation
logs (\S\ref{sec:protocol:enja}), the analyses that are not part of the preregistration
(\S\ref{sec:protocol:exploratory}) and how the registered plan relates to what was run
(\S\ref{sec:protocol:registration}). No pass or fail criterion is set: $k$, $N$ and the levels of processing are
settings of the experiment, not thresholds.

\paragraph{Status of the analyses.}
The threat model, the forms, the attackers, their parameters and the analyses of Sections~\ref{sec:threat-model}
to~\ref{sec:protocol} were fixed in the preregistration of this study before any data were acquired; what the authors of
KLiCKe told us about the competition data (\S\ref{sec:threat-model:settings}) was learned afterwards. The
exceptions are the deviations listed in \S\ref{sec:protocol:registration} and the five analyses of
\S\ref{sec:protocol:exploratory}, which are labelled as exploratory wherever they are reported. These sections
motivate and state the registered plan and do not change it.

\subsection{Candidates, scope and samples}
\label{sec:protocol:candidates}

\paragraph{Full texts.}
For each log, the true text is the distributed text of its writer. The $N - 1$ decoys are drawn uniformly without
replacement from the distributed texts of all other writers of the same prompt after exclusion. Because attackers are
trained on the other two prompts, no text of a training writer is a candidate. We use $N = 10$, $100$ and $1{,}000$,
with 20 draws of the decoys per log for $N = 10$ and $100$ and 5 draws for $N = 1{,}000$. If the true text is among
$m$ candidates that tie, the log counts $1/m$. A prompt with fewer than $N$ writers after exclusion has no cell for
that $N$.

\paragraph{Windows.}
Windows contain $k = 5$, $10$, $20$ or $50$ non-empty segments (\S\ref{sec:attackers:windows}), with five window starts
per log. The true window, the $k$ words of the text that the window's presses became, is found once on the unprocessed
log and used by every form and attacker; each decoy contributes one window of $k$ words. The rules for valid windows
are in Appendix~\ref{app:attackers}.

\paragraph{Samples.}
KeyTAR is expensive to run, so 125 writers are drawn from each prompt, 500 in all, and the main tables report every
attacker on these 500 writers. Attackers A0 to A3 on all writers after exclusion are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:results}.

\subsection{Reported quantities}
\label{sec:protocol:quantities}

For each form, attacker, $N$ and scope we report the accuracy (the share of logs whose true text is chosen, averaged
over draws and starts) with its 95\% interval, its multiple of $1/N$, and its gain over A0, the difference in accuracy
from the attacker that uses only the total length, with its interval. Intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals with
writers as units, 2,000 resamples stratified by prompt; cells of the same sample use the same resamples. Two
reference points serve different purposes. What processing removes is read from R0 and from the contrasts between
forms, as the protection of a release is measured against the unprocessed data~\cite{stadler2022synthetic}; the gain
over A0 is what an attacker obtains beyond the total length that every form leaves.

\paragraph{Strongest attacker.}
For each form, the strongest attacker is the maximum over the adaptive attackers available on that form. Its interval
takes the maximum again within each bootstrap resample, and we report the share of resamples in which each attacker is
selected. It is the maximum over the attackers tried and not an attack that combines all length and timing information;
only A3 on R3-u combines the two. KeyTAR is reported separately (Appendix~\ref{app:results}).

\paragraph{Contrasts between forms.}
We report the difference in the strongest attacker's accuracy, with its interval, for adjacent forms: R1~$-$~R2 (from
masking to categories, which also drops the activity and the text change), R2~$-$~R3 (dropping the position and the word
count together), R3~$-$~R3-u (separators) and R3~$-$~R3-t($w$) (rounding of times). The first two change several columns
at once. The last two change one column, but the available attackers change too: in R3-u, A2-b replaces A1$'$ and A2 and
A3 uses the combined score, and in R3-t the likelihood of A3 uses rounding intervals. These differences therefore
contain both the column and the attacker. Attackers that do not use times give a difference of zero between R3 and
R3-t, so the difference of A3 is also reported. No form drops all times; the coarsest rounding, $w = 1{,}000$\,ms,
stands in for it.

\paragraph{Secondary quantities.}
The rank of the true text, the distribution of accuracy per writer to its 90th percentile and the other secondary
quantities are listed and reported in Appendix~\ref{app:results}.

\subsection{Utility}
\label{sec:protocol:utility}

A form that defeats matching may also defeat the research the release was for. We measure this with the ten process
measures of the KLiCKe paper~\cite{tian2025klicke}: mean P-burst length (bursts ended by a pause over 2\,s), mean
R-burst length, mean pause (intervals over 200\,ms), mean within-word and between-word pauses, mean deletion length,
deletion proportion, mean insertion length, insertion proportion, and the variance of the production rate over ten equal
intervals (named as in that paper and computed as a standard deviation, below). The definitions follow that paper and are computed with our own formulas, the same code for every form. The
paper gives no operational definition of the ``task maximum'' to which the production rate is related; we divide the
production rate of each interval by the maximum interval rate over all writers of the same prompt, which is the same
denominator for every form, and take the standard deviation over the ten intervals. Characters are counted as character
presses, word boundaries as separator presses and pauses as intervals between presses, in the unprocessed log as well.

Before any data were seen, we fixed whether each measure is computed on each form with the same
inputs and formula as in R0 (ok), from other inputs by approximation rules (approx), or not at all (none); the table is in Appendix~\ref{app:results}, and Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the counts for each form. The
approximation rules are: the number of characters is the number of character presses; a deletion is one character per
BackSpace or Delete press; an insertion is a character press while the cursor of the A1$'$ replay is not at the end; a
word boundary is a separator press; and counts in R7 are scaled by $1/(1-p)$. The random processing of R5, R6 and R7
is applied once per log, and the same processed logs are used for matching and for utility.

For each measure and form we report the Spearman correlation with the value in R0; the median and the 10th and 90th
percentiles of the ratio to the value in R0, leaving out writers whose R0 value is zero or undefined (with their
numbers); and the Spearman correlation with the human scores, for R0 and each form side by side. Each is reported per
prompt, with the mean and range over the four prompts, and with bootstrap intervals on the same resamples as the
matching cells of the same sample.

The registered plan included a check of our formulas against the values computed by Inputlog for 50 writers. The corpus
does not distribute these values, and we did not run Inputlog ourselves, so the check could not be done and our
formulas are not verified against Inputlog. One consequence is that our deletion and insertion runs are broken by rows
of modifier keys between them, as the registered wording reads; this may differ from what the KLiCKe paper counts as
one deletion activity.

\subsection{Summary table and figure}
\label{sec:protocol:summary}

The summary table covers full texts and the 500 writers, with one table for $N = 1{,}000$ and one for $N = 10$, the
small setting of the threat model. Its rows are the main forms (R0, R1, R2, R3, R3-u, R3-t($w$), R4($w$), R7($p$)), and
its columns the accuracy of A0, the strongest attacker's accuracy and interval, the selected attacker with the shares
of resamples, the multiple of $1/N$, the gain over A0, the numbers of process measures that are computed, approximated
and not computed, and the median Spearman correlation with R0 over the ten measures. The figure plots, for $N =
1{,}000$ on full texts, the strongest attacker's multiple of $1/N$ against the median Spearman correlation with R0 over
the ten measures, in which a measure that is not computed counts as zero and an approximated measure counts with its
approximate value; R4 computes none of the measures and therefore sits at zero. The utility axis is the agreement of
our own formulas with their values in R0, for these ten measures only; it does not cover measures not yet defined or
the prediction of scores, and it depends on how the measures are aggregated. The forms that are not dominated on both
axes are described, against the attackers tried. All cells are given in the appendix, and no cell is selected for being favourable.

\subsection{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:protocol:diagnostics}

\paragraph{Negative control.}
Each log is replaced by the log of another writer of the same prompt, drawn at random, and matched in the same way,
with the true text unchanged and the decoys drawn from the writers other than these two. This is done for the main
forms, $N = 10$ and $1{,}000$, full texts and the 500 writers. The attacker is fixed to the one selected for the same
form and $N$ in the main computation and is not selected again, since the maximum over attackers that are all at chance
lies above chance; each adaptive attacker is also reported. Accuracy near $1/N$ is expected.

\paragraph{Open world.}
For $N = 1{,}000$ on full texts, each draw gives two values for the strongest attacker: its lowest cost over the $N$
candidates, and its lowest cost over the same candidates without the true text. Pooling each kind of value over all
logs and draws, we report the probability that a value of the first kind is lower than a value of the second kind,
over all pairs (ties count one half), as an area under the curve. It shows how well the scores separate candidate sets that
contain the true text from sets that do not; it is not a rate of false matches (\S\ref{sec:threat-model:bias}). Since
costs grow with the length of a log, the pooled area also contains the differences in length between logs and can show
less separation than a decision made within each log~\cite{narayanan2008robust}. A
diagnostic of the position of true windows is in Appendix~\ref{app:attackers}.

\subsection{Japanese translation logs}
\label{sec:protocol:enja}

The fourth question of the study asks how far above chance and above A0 the same kind of matching is on the Japanese
translation logs. The candidates for a log are translations of the same source text by other translators ($N$ = all,
and $N = 10$). The quantity that answers the question is the accuracy of A2 on R3j, full texts and $N$ = all, with its
differences from $1/N$ and from A0, and we say that matching ``exceeds'' chance and A0 only if the lower bounds of the
intervals of both differences are above zero. The averaging and the intervals, which resample translators since
translators and source texts are crossed, are given in Appendix~\ref{app:enja}. ENJA15 is much smaller than KLiCKe,
and its intervals are expected to be wide.

\subsection{Exploratory analyses not in the preregistration}
\label{sec:protocol:exploratory}

Five analyses were added after the preregistration was fixed and are not part of it: the uniqueness of word-length
sequences, a form with differentially private noise, the upper tail of accuracy per writer, matches above a margin of
cost, and accuracy by score band. They are reported as exploratory, are not used to answer the four questions, and
are given with their procedures in Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory}.

\subsection{Registration, deviations and reproducibility}
\label{sec:protocol:registration}

\paragraph{Registration.}
The plan, its parameters and its seed were registered before any data were acquired, and no registered value was
changed; the registered files, their SHA-256 and how each random choice derives its seed are given in the open science
section.

\paragraph{Deviations and predictions.}
Four deviations are recorded with their times and reasons, and none changed the released columns or a registered
value. Predictions of the strongest attacker's accuracy were registered before any data were acquired; the
registration states that a miss does not change the conclusions. Both are given in Appendix~\ref{app:checks}.
