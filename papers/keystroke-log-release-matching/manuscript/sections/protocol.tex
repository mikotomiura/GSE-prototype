\section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

This section states how candidate sets are built (\S\ref{sec:protocol:candidates}), what is reported for matching
(\S\ref{sec:protocol:quantities}) and for the process measures (\S\ref{sec:protocol:utility}), how the two are
summarised (\S\ref{sec:protocol:summary}), the diagnostics (\S\ref{sec:protocol:diagnostics}) and the analysis of the
Japanese translation logs (\S\ref{sec:protocol:enja}). Five exploratory analyses that are not part of the
preregistration are described separately (\S\ref{sec:protocol:exploratory}). Section~\ref{sec:protocol:registration}
states how the registered plan relates to what was run. No pass or fail criterion is set: $k$, $N$ and the levels of
processing are settings of the experiment, not thresholds.

\subsection{Candidates, scope and samples}
\label{sec:protocol:candidates}

\paragraph{Full texts.}
For each log, the true text is the distributed text of its writer. The $N - 1$ decoys are drawn uniformly without
replacement from the distributed texts of all other writers of the same prompt after exclusion. Because attackers are
trained on the other two prompts, no text of a training writer is a candidate. We use $N = 10$, $100$ and $1{,}000$,
with 20 draws of the decoys per log for $N = 10$ and $100$ and 5 draws for $N = 1{,}000$. If the true text is among
$m$ candidates that tie, the log counts $1/m$. A prompt with fewer than $N$ writers after exclusion has no cell for
that $N$.

\paragraph{Windows.}
Windows contain $k = 5$, $10$, $20$ or $50$ non-empty segments (\S\ref{sec:attackers:windows}), with five window starts
per log drawn uniformly from the valid starts. The true window is found once, on the unprocessed log, and used by
every form and attacker. Replaying the log gives, for each press, the character of the final draft that it became; the
true window is the $k$ words of the text that start at the word holding most of the surviving characters of the
window's first segment. Windows whose first segment leaves no character in the final draft, and windows that would run
past the end of the text, are not used. For writers whose rebuilt draft does not match the distributed text, the words
of the two are aligned after normalisation of whitespace, and only windows whose first word maps to a word of the
distributed text are used; the windows that do not map are counted. Each decoy contributes one window of $k$ words,
drawn uniformly from its valid windows. Logs with no valid window of length $k$ are left out for that $k$, and their
number is reported.

\paragraph{Samples.}
KeyTAR is expensive to run, so 125 writers are drawn from each prompt, 500 in all, and the main tables report every
attacker on these 500 writers, so that KeyTAR stands next to the other attackers on the same writers. Attackers A0 to A3 on all writers after exclusion are reported in the appendix.

\subsection{Reported quantities}
\label{sec:protocol:quantities}

For each form, attacker, $N$ and scope we report the accuracy (the share of logs whose true text is chosen, averaged
over draws and starts) with its 95\% interval, its multiple of $1/N$, and its gain over A0, the difference in accuracy
from the attacker that uses only the total length, with its interval. Intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals with
writers as units, 2,000 resamples stratified by prompt; cells of the same sample use the same resamples. Two
reference points serve different purposes. What processing removes is read from R0 and from the contrasts between
forms, as the protection of a release is measured against the unprocessed data~\cite{stadler2022synthetic}; the gain
over A0 is what an attacker obtains beyond the total length that every form leaves.

\paragraph{Strongest attacker.}
For each form, the strongest attacker is the maximum over the adaptive attackers available on that form. Its interval
takes the maximum again within each bootstrap resample, and we report the share of resamples in which each attacker is
selected. It is the maximum over the attackers tried and not an attack that combines all length and timing information;
only A3 on R3-u combines the two. KeyTAR is reported in a separate row.

\paragraph{Contrasts between forms.}
We report the difference in the strongest attacker's accuracy, with its interval, for adjacent forms: R1~$-$~R2 (from
masking to categories, which also drops the activity and the text change), R2~$-$~R3 (dropping the position and the word
count together), R3~$-$~R3-u (separators) and R3~$-$~R3-t($w$) (rounding of times). The first two change several columns
at once. The last two change one column, but the available attackers change too: in R3-u, A2-b replaces A1$'$ and A2 and
A3 uses the combined score, and in R3-t the likelihood of A3 uses rounding intervals. These differences therefore
contain both the column and the attacker. Attackers that do not use times give a difference of zero between R3 and
R3-t, so the difference of A3 is also reported. No form drops all times; the coarsest rounding, $w = 1{,}000$\,ms,
stands in for it.

\paragraph{Secondary quantities.}
We also report the rank of the true text (top-5 accuracy and mean reciprocal rank); the distribution of accuracy per
writer, at its 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles; A2 with $\lambda = 4$ and $16$ ($N = 1{,}000$, full text); accuracy
separately for writers whose rebuilt draft matches the distributed text and writers whose draft does not; and the
numbers of candidates without an alignment path.

\subsection{Utility}
\label{sec:protocol:utility}

A form that defeats matching may also defeat the research the release was for. We measure this with the ten process
measures of the KLiCKe paper~\cite{tian2025klicke}: mean P-burst length (bursts ended by a pause over 2\,s), mean
R-burst length, mean pause (intervals over 200\,ms), mean within-word and between-word pauses, mean deletion length,
deletion proportion, mean insertion length, insertion proportion, and the variance of the production rate over ten equal
intervals (named as in that paper and computed as a standard deviation, below). The definitions follow that paper and are computed with our own formulas, the same code for every form. The
paper gives no operational definition of the ``task maximum'' to which the production rate is related; we divide the
production rate of each interval by the maximum interval rate over all writers of the same prompt, which is the same
denominator for every form, and take the standard deviation over the ten intervals. Characters are counted as character
presses, word boundaries as separator presses and pauses as intervals between presses, in the unprocessed log as well.

Table~\ref{tab:utility} fixes, before any data were seen, whether each measure is computed on each form with the same
inputs and formula as in R0 (ok), from other inputs by approximation rules (approx), or not at all (none). The
approximation rules are: the number of characters is the number of character presses; a deletion is one character per
BackSpace or Delete press; an insertion is a character press while the cursor of the A1$'$ replay is not at the end; a
word boundary is a separator press; and counts in R7 are scaled by $1/(1-p)$. The random processing of R5, R6 and R7
is applied once per log, and the same processed logs are used for matching and for utility.

For each measure and form we report the Spearman correlation with the value in R0; the median and the 10th and 90th
percentiles of the ratio to the value in R0, leaving out writers whose R0 value is zero or undefined (with their
numbers); and the Spearman correlation with the human scores, for R0 and each form side by side. Each is reported per
prompt, with the mean and range over the four prompts, and with bootstrap intervals on the same resamples as the
matching cells of the same sample.

The registered plan included a check of our formulas against the values computed by Inputlog for 50 writers. The corpus
does not distribute these values, and we did not run Inputlog ourselves, so the check could not be done and our
formulas are not verified against Inputlog. One consequence is that our deletion and insertion runs are broken by rows
of modifier keys between them, as the registered wording reads; this may differ from what the KLiCKe paper counts as
one deletion activity.

\subsection{Summary table and figure}
\label{sec:protocol:summary}

The summary table covers full texts and the 500 writers, with one table for $N = 1{,}000$ and one for $N = 10$, the
small setting of the threat model. Its rows are the main forms (R0, R1, R2, R3, R3-u, R3-t($w$), R4($w$), R7($p$)), and
its columns the accuracy of A0, the strongest attacker's accuracy and interval, the selected attacker with the shares
of resamples, the multiple of $1/N$, the gain over A0, the numbers of process measures that are computed, approximated
and not computed, and the median Spearman correlation with R0 over the ten measures. The figure plots, for $N =
1{,}000$ on full texts, the strongest attacker's multiple of $1/N$ against the median Spearman correlation with R0 over
the ten measures, in which a measure that is not computed counts as zero and an approximated measure counts with its
approximate value; R4 computes none of the measures and therefore sits at zero. The utility axis is the agreement of
our own formulas with their values in R0, for these ten measures only; it does not cover measures not yet defined or
the prediction of scores, and it depends on how the measures are aggregated. The forms that are not dominated on both
axes are described, against the attackers tried; a low accuracy is not evidence of protection
(\S\ref{sec:threat-model:bias}). All cells are given in the appendix, and no cell is selected for being favourable. The table
and the figure are material for choosing a form; no rule recommends one, and the decision is left to the releasing
party.

\subsection{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:protocol:diagnostics}

\paragraph{Negative control.}
Each log is replaced by the log of another writer of the same prompt, drawn at random, and matched in the same way,
with the true text unchanged and the decoys drawn from the writers other than these two. This is done for the main
forms, $N = 10$ and $1{,}000$, full texts and the 500 writers. The attacker is fixed to the one selected for the same
form and $N$ in the main computation and is not selected again, since the maximum over attackers that are all at chance
lies above chance; each adaptive attacker is also reported. Accuracy near $1/N$ is expected.

\paragraph{Open world.}
For $N = 1{,}000$ on full texts, each draw gives two values for the strongest attacker: its lowest cost over the $N$
candidates, and its lowest cost over the same candidates without the true text. Pooling each kind of value over all
logs and draws, we report the probability that a value of the first kind is lower than a value of the second kind,
over all pairs (ties count one half), as an area under the curve. It shows how well the scores separate candidate sets that
contain the true text from sets that do not; it is not a rate of false matches (\S\ref{sec:threat-model:bias}).

\paragraph{Position of true windows.}
To see whether true windows sit at positions that favour the alignment, we show the distribution of the A2 alignment
cost at the true window next to that of the whole log.

\subsection{Japanese translation logs}
\label{sec:protocol:enja}

The fourth question of the study asks how far above chance and above A0 the same kind of matching is on the Japanese
translation logs. The candidates for a log are translations of the same source text by other translators: all of them
($N$ = all), or nine drawn without replacement, 20 times per log ($N = 10$); identical translations tie. Accuracy, $1/N$
and the accuracy of A0 are averaged over the logs of each source text and then over the source texts. Since
translators and source texts are crossed, intervals come from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of translators with all their
sessions, without stratification, and differences are taken within each resample. The quantity that answers the
question is the accuracy of A2 on R3j, full texts and $N$ = all, with its differences from $1/N$ and from A0. We say
that matching ``exceeds'' chance and A0 only if the lower bounds of the intervals of both differences are above zero.
R3j-t($w$) and R4($w$) are reported with the same rule; since neither A0 nor A2 uses times, R3j-t($w$) gives the
values of R3j. Values per source text are only described, and R0j is a reference. ENJA15 is much smaller than KLiCKe, and its intervals are expected to be wide.

\subsection{Exploratory analyses not in the preregistration}
\label{sec:protocol:exploratory}

% The statement about the last three analyses holds only if no result of the main computation had been opened when
% their procedures were committed; confirm this with the record of the main computation before submission.
Five analyses were added after the preregistration was fixed and are not part of it. They change no registered value
or procedure. Their procedures were fixed and committed to the study repository before any value of matching or
utility on the human data had been seen, the first two on 13 October 2026 and the other three on 21 October 2026, and
they are reported as exploratory and are not used to answer the four questions. The last three use the strongest
attacker selected in the main computation for the same form and $N$, without selecting again, on full texts, the main
forms, $N = 10$ and $1{,}000$ and the 500 writers, with the same resamples.

\paragraph{Uniqueness of word-length sequences among $N$ candidates.}
The first analysis uses the texts alone. For each of the 500 writers and each $k$ from 1 to 50, five runs of $k$
consecutive words are drawn from the writer's text, and we report the share for which none of the $N - 1$ decoys,
drawn as in the main computation, contains the same sequence of word lengths at any position; as a secondary quantity,
the accuracy $1/m$ of an attacker who chooses uniformly among the $m$ texts that contain it. Next to these curves we
draw reference lines $k^{*} = \log_2 N / h$, where $h$ is a plug-in estimate of the entropy rate of word lengths in
bits per word, conditioned on up to two preceding words~\cite{kalimeri2012entropy}; plug-in estimates fall as the
order rises, and the lines are a reference, not a prediction. We call the quantity uniqueness among $N$ candidates and
not a risk of re-identification. Whether uniqueness in a sample says anything about a population is
disputed~\cite{sanchez2016comment,rocher2019estimating}, and in location data unicity decreases, slowly, as the dataset
grows~\cite{farzanehfar2021risk}; here the population is the set of $N$ candidates itself, and we do not extend the
quantity beyond the values of $N$ tried.

\paragraph{A form with differentially private noise.}
The second analysis adds a form R-DP($\varepsilon$), $\varepsilon = 0.5$, $1$, $2$, $4$, built on R3, with letters and
digits released as one category. Two mechanisms use the same $\varepsilon$. First, the number of counted letter and
digit presses of each segment receives two-sided geometric noise with sensitivity 1, and letter presses are added at
random positions in the segment or removed at random accordingly, with the count kept at zero or above. Second, every
press interval is clipped at 2,000\,ms, the threshold of the P-bursts, and receives two-sided geometric noise with
sensitivity 2,000\,ms, and negative intervals are set to zero. Each guarantee holds for its own unit, one segment count
or one interval, and not for a writer's whole log: over a log the budgets add up over all segments and
intervals~\cite{kellaris2014event}. We do not claim that the combined release is differentially private for neighbouring
logs that differ by an inserted or removed press with its time. The noise is drawn from a seeded generator so that the
evaluation can be repeated, which is acceptable for evaluation but not for an actual release~\cite{holohan2023random},
and an actual release would also need an implementation that is exact on finite
computers~\cite{canonne2020discrete}. The attackers are those of R3, with the timing table of A3 built from training
logs with the same interval noise; the candidates are unchanged, and every process measure that R3 computes is counted as
approximated. The scope, samples and resamples are those of the main computation, with the contrast R3~$-$~R-DP and the
open-world area, but no negative control. R-DP is reported in exploratory tables, where $\varepsilon$ is labelled as the budget per segment count and per
interval, and it is not part of the main table or figure.

\paragraph{The upper tail of accuracy per writer.}
The third analysis reports the 95th and 99th percentiles of accuracy per writer and the share of writers whose true text
is chosen alone in every draw. No value per writer is reported.

\paragraph{Matches above a margin.}
The fourth analysis takes, for each log and draw, the difference between the attacker's second-lowest and lowest cost
over the candidates (zero if the lowest is tied), with log-likelihoods turned into costs by a change of sign. At
thresholds set at the 0th, 10th, \ldots, 90th percentiles of these differences, it reports the share of logs and draws
at or above the threshold and the accuracy among them. Costs of different attackers are not compared.

\paragraph{Accuracy by score band.}
The fifth analysis divides the 500 writers of each prompt into thirds by their human score and reports the strongest
attacker's accuracy in each third and the difference between the top and bottom thirds. The attackers do not use the
scores.

\subsection{Registration, deviations and reproducibility}
\label{sec:protocol:registration}

\paragraph{Registration.}
The plan, its parameters (\texttt{config.json}) and its seed (\texttt{SEED}) were registered before any data were
acquired, with the SHA-256 of each file (see the open science section). The present \texttt{config.json} differs from
the registered file only in three fields that the registered file left to be filled at acquisition: the version and
SHA-256 of the files acquired from each source. No registered value was changed. Every random choice uses a seed
derived as the first eight bytes of the SHA-256 of the registered seed joined with the use, prompt, writer, $N$, $k$ and
draw, so each draw can be reproduced on its own.

\paragraph{Deviations.}
% Update the list of deviations from the record when the main computation is complete.
At the time of writing, two deviations are recorded with their times and reasons. The first is the undoing of escaped characters when the logs
are read (\S\ref{sec:data:checks}). The second concerns the code: three processing functions removed a column that
the reading of KLiCKe had already dropped, which stopped a trial run; they were changed to remove the column only if
it is present. The released columns did not change, and no value of matching or utility had been
computed before either change. Details that the registration left open were decided, before the data were opened or
before any value of matching was seen, and are recorded with the code.

\paragraph{Predictions.}
Before any data were acquired, we registered predictions of the strongest attacker's accuracy for $N = 1{,}000$ on full
texts, and of KeyTAR and of the forms reported in the appendix, with a reason for each; they were proposed by an AI assistant and adopted by the authors (see the AI use
section). The registration states that a miss does not change the conclusions. They are given with the results
(Section~\ref{sec:results}) only to show what was expected before the data were seen.
