\section{Data and release forms}
\label{sec:data}

% What this part is meant to establish and what is measured. What was found and why it matters are written in

The question for each release form is how far it keeps a log apart from its text and what it costs research. This
section fixes the forms (\S\ref{sec:data:forms}) and the data on which they are measured (\S\ref{sec:data:corpora}):
for each form, the evaluation measures the accuracy of matching by attackers that know the form
(Sections~\ref{sec:attackers} and~\ref{sec:protocol}) and how far ten process measures of writing research can still
be computed (\S\ref{sec:protocol:utility}).

\subsection{Corpora and model}
\label{sec:data:corpora}

\paragraph{KLiCKe.}
The KLiCKe corpus holds the keystroke logs, final texts and human scores of 4,992 argumentative essays on four prompts
derived from the SAT, with 1,136 to 1,329 writers per prompt, each essay written in 30 minutes~\cite{tian2025klicke}.
A~log has one row per event, with the press and release times, the action time, the pressed and released keys, the
activity (such as input, removal, paste or replacement), the text change, the cursor position and the word count. The
description of use by the corpus authors allows the files to be downloaded and used freely, with the corpus repository
cited as the source~\cite{inputlog2026community}. That repository, which links to the data held elsewhere, carries an
MIT License file~\cite{klicke2025repo}. The corpus was acquired after the preregistration was registered (see the open
science section), and its demographic file was not acquired (see the ethical considerations).

Writers are excluded if their log, text or score is missing, or if their text has fewer than 50 words (runs of letters
and digits). No writer met a criterion, and all 4,992 writers remain.
Each log is trimmed to its active writing window, from the first key press to the last event that changes the text in
the unprocessed log; a releasing party would apply such trimming before processing, and the corpus authors also remove
inactivity at the start and end~\cite{tian2025klicke}. The four prompts are split with a fixed seed into two pairs.
Attackers that need training are trained on the writers of one pair and evaluated on the writers of the other, and then
the roles are swapped. Every writer is thus evaluated once, and no writer or text of an evaluated prompt is used for
training.

\paragraph{ENJA15.}
ENJA15 is a public corpus of English-to-Japanese translation processes recorded with Translog-II, in which Japanese was
typed with an input method~\cite{carl2016lrec,zou2026translog}. We use its 76 from-scratch translation sessions, by 39
translators on six source texts, with the final translation recorded in each log as its text; after exclusion
all 76 sessions remain. Its licence, the exclusion criteria and the design are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:enja}.

\paragraph{The KeyTAR model.}
We also run the public KeyTAR model~\cite{qiu2026keytar} as a reference outside the threat model
(Appendix~\ref{app:attackers}).

\paragraph{Checks after acquisition.}
Before any value of matching or utility was computed, the preregistration allowed only a fixed list of checks, which
report counts only (Appendix~\ref{app:checks}). One led to a deviation: the distributed logs write four characters as
two-character escape sequences, which the procedure did not anticipate, and we undo them when the logs are read. After
this, the final draft rebuilt from the unprocessed log matches the distributed text for 4,842 of the 4,992 writers
(97.0\%); accuracy is also reported separately for writers whose rebuilt draft matches and writers whose draft does not.

\subsection{Release forms}
\label{sec:data:forms}

A release form fixes, for each column, what is released. Table~\ref{tab:forms} lists the forms. R0 is the unprocessed
log, a reference. R1 is the masking of the competition data~\cite{kaggle2023lwp}. R2 to R4 release less and less: key
categories with the position and word count, key categories alone, categories with characters and separators merged,
and the sequence of segment values alone. R3-t rounds the press times and R7 suppresses presses at random. Two further
forms, R5 (substitution) and R6 (permutation), are our definitions after the names of processing steps in Vassallo et
al.~\cite{vassallo2017privacy}; they are defined and reported in Appendix~\ref{app:results}. The masking with
\texttt{@} of the programming datasets~\cite{edwards2023csedm} is not a form here, since it hides names and not the
text.

\paragraph{What the coarser forms keep.}
Forms R3, R3-u, R3-t and R7 release the event number, the press time and the category of the pressed key, and nothing
else. Release times and hold times are dropped because they carry the duration of a drag or of a held key, and the
released key is dropped because a released key that differs from the pressed key could reveal the order of releases.
Columns that the table does not list, such as the pause time distributed with KLiCKe, are dropped in every form except
R0, and no form keeps a column derived from times. R3-t differs from R3 only in its times, so attackers that do not
use times give the same values in both. In R7 the remaining events are renumbered. A drag's duration, and that of a
held key when the next press follows its release, still remains as a lower bound of the next press interval, which a
form that releases press times cannot remove. These definitions follow checks on synthetic logs made before
registration (Appendix~\ref{app:checks:synthetic}).

\paragraph{Forms for ENJA15.}
For ENJA15 the forms are R0j (no processing); R3j, which replaces each key event by one of eight categories and
removes every string; R3j-t($w$), with rounded press times; and R4($w$) (Appendix~\ref{app:enja}).
