\begin{abstract}
Pause and stall labels in keystroke logs rest on choices that differ between studies: a window, a threshold, a
condition for a stop and a segmentation of the log. We asked which conclusions
move across definitions. We applied a window-based stall detector to four datasets released for research (two
editions of an introductory programming course, an essay corpus and a corpus of writing with model suggestions),
varied its four choices over 89 definitions around its default, added a second detector that counts
inter-key intervals above a fixed threshold of 2 to 10~s (18 definitions, and 18 with a writer quantile as a
control for the rate of key presses), with intervals from resampling writers.
By criteria set before the computations, the association between the stall rate and the holistic essay score was
present for most definitions, but its direction changed: it was positive only for ten definitions combining a
15-s window (a level without precedent) with a writer quantile. Among the fixed pause thresholds both the presence and
the direction of the association moved, with values mostly negligible (13 of 18 within $\pm 0.1$). Adjusting for
essay length, which correlated with the score at 0.75, left no stall definition negative and most values negligible;
an exploratory adjustment for typing speed left all stall definitions negative. Between the programming editions
the stall-rate ratio reversed for a fifth of the definitions, below a criterion set after seeing point values;
within-writer overdispersion depended on the definition, and the largest component of stall length stayed. We propose,
after seeing these results, to report the range of each conclusion across definitions and adjustments with intervals,
and to read it as consistent, negligible or dependent on the definition; code and aggregate tables are released.\\

{\parindent0pt
\textbf{Keywords:} keystroke logging, pauses, stalls, multiverse analysis, introductory programming, writing
}
\end{abstract}
