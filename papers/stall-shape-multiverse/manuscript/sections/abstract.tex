\begin{abstract}
Pause and stall labels in keystroke logs rest on choices that differ between studies: a window, a threshold, a
condition for a stop and a segmentation of the log. We asked which conclusions
move across definitions. We applied a window-based stall detector to four datasets released for research (two
editions of an introductory programming course, an essay corpus and a corpus of writing with model suggestions),
varied its four choices over 89 definitions around its default, added a second detector that counts
inter-key intervals above a fixed threshold of 2 to 10~s (18 definitions, and 18 with a writer quantile as a
control for the rate of key presses), with intervals from resampling writers.
By criteria set before the computations, the association between the stall rate and the holistic essay score was
present for most definitions, but its direction changed: it was positive only for ten definitions combining a 15-s
window, half the default, with a writer quantile, and on a grid of default and precedented levels it was negative or
negligible. Among fixed pause thresholds the values were mostly negligible (13 of 18 within $\pm 0.1$), and the
direction changed between them. Adjusting for essay length, which correlated with the score at 0.75, left no stall
definition negative and most values negligible but raised half of the pause definitions above $+0.1$. Rate ratios
between the programming editions and within-writer overdispersion also moved with the definition, while the largest
component of stall length stayed. We propose, after seeing these results, to report the range of each conclusion
across definitions and adjustments with intervals, and to read it as consistent, negligible, dependent on the
definition or imprecise; code and aggregate tables are released.\\

{\parindent0pt
\textbf{Keywords:} keystroke logging, pauses, stalls, multiverse analysis, introductory programming, writing
}
\end{abstract}
