\begin{abstract}
Pauses, stalls and inactivity in keystroke logs are widely used process measures, but every such label rests on
choices that differ between studies: the window, the threshold, the condition for a stop and the segmentation of
observation. We asked which conclusions drawn from a stall label move across defensible definitions and which stay. We
applied one detector to four public datasets (two editions of an introductory programming course, an essay corpus and
a corpus of writing with model suggestions), varied the four choices over 89 definitions around a baseline, and
reported each conclusion with an interval from resampling writers. The strength of the association between the stall
rate and essay score (one holistic score in one corpus; proportions are of the definitions examined) moved from
negligible ($\rho = -0.06$ under the baseline) to small-to-moderate (as large as $-0.25$), and the direction split
within one family of thresholds: in the corner with the shortest window the association was weakly positive for 10
definitions. With the same diagnostic, the ratio of stall rates between the programming course and the essay corpus
reversed beyond sampling variation for most definitions and within-writer overdispersion of counts depended on the
definition, whereas the dominance of stall-to-stall variation in stall length in the programming data stayed. Adjusted
for typing speed, an exploratory analysis, the association was negative for nearly all definitions. Reports of stall
labels should give the range across definitions with intervals; the detector, the grid and the aggregate tables are
released.\\

{\parindent0pt
\textbf{Keywords:} keystroke logging, pauses, stalls, multiverse analysis, introductory programming, writing
}
\end{abstract}
