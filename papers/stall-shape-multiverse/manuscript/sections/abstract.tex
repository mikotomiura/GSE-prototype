\begin{abstract}
Pause and stall labels in keystroke logs rest on choices that differ between studies: a window, a threshold, a
condition for a stop and a segmentation of the log. We asked which conclusions drawn from such a label move across
definitions and which stay. We applied a window-based stall detector to four datasets released for research (two
editions of an introductory programming course, an essay corpus and a corpus of writing with model suggestions),
varied its four choices over 89 definitions around its default, added a second detector that counts
inter-key intervals above a fixed threshold of 2 to 10~s (18 definitions; 18 with a writer quantile served as a
control, since their rate follows the rate of key presses), and gave each conclusion an interval from resampling writers.
By criteria set before the computations, the association between the label rate and the holistic essay score was
present for most definitions, but its direction changed: for stalls only with a 15-s window, a level without
precedent, and for pauses from a negligible positive correlation at 2~s ($\leq 0.05$) to a small negative one at
10~s (down to $-0.13$). Adjusting for essay length, which correlated with the score at 0.75, left no definition with a
negative association; an exploratory adjustment for typing speed left all stall definitions negative. Between the two
programming editions, the ratio of stall rates reversed for a fifth of the definitions, mostly when the threshold
became fixed, and within-writer overdispersion of counts depended on the definition. Reports of pause and stall rates
should give the range across definitions and adjustments, with intervals; the code and the aggregate tables are
released.\\

{\parindent0pt
\textbf{Keywords:} keystroke logging, pauses, stalls, multiverse analysis, introductory programming, writing
}
\end{abstract}
