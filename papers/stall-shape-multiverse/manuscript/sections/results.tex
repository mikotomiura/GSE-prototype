\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The direction of the label--essay-score association changes within each detector}
\label{sec:res-score}

A study that reports how a pause or stall rate relates to the quality of a product usually reports one definition. We
asked whether that association depends on the definition, and measured, for each definition, the Spearman
correlation between the KLiCKe writers' rates and their holistic scores, with a resampled interval
(Figure~\ref{fig:score}, Table~\ref{tab:pause}).

\paragraph{Stalls.} Under the baseline the correlation was $-0.063$ [$-0.091$, $-0.034$]. Two criteria had been set
before the association was seen. The presence of an association would count as moving if the share of definitions
with an interval excluding zero lay between 25\% and 75\%; it was 82 of 89 (92\%), so by that criterion presence did not
move. The direction would count as changing if one definition had its point value and its interval on the other side
of zero; 10 did, so the direction changed. All 10 had the shortest window, $W = 15$~s, the level we set at half the
default and for which we found no precedent, and a writer-quantile $\theta$; among the 59 definitions with other
windows, 57 were negative and none was positive. Across the 89 definitions the correlation ranged from $-0.25$ to
$+0.14$ (median $-0.14$), with intervals negative for 72, positive for 10 and including zero for 7. In strength, 58
definitions gave a negative correlation of at least 0.1 in absolute value and 3 a positive one; all 36 fixed-second
definitions were negative. The positive corner is not a floor effect of rare stalls: the share of writers with no stall
was 0.1--1.3\% there, against 0.1--35\% across all 89. These are pooled over the four prompts; within prompts, as point
values only, the median correlation was negative for 74 definitions and positive for 15 (S3).

\paragraph{Pauses.} The second detector, with thresholds in prior use, gave the same kind of result at a smaller
size. With fixed thresholds, the correlation had an interval below zero for all six definitions at 10~s ($-0.13$ to
$-0.06$) and for one of the six at 5~s, and above zero for two of the three definitions on character intervals at 2~s
($+0.045$ and $+0.049$) and one at 5~s; the other eight included zero. By the same criterion the direction changed,
from negligible positive values to small negative ones. The negative values at 10~s are smaller in magnitude than the
$-0.15$ to $-0.43$ reported for pause durations and latencies, a different measure (Section~\ref{sec:related}). The 18 definitions with a writer-quantile threshold, a control
for the rate of key presses (Section~\ref{sec:grid}), correlated with the score at $+0.58$ to $+0.67$, close to the
0.62 of the rate of key presses itself. The reading set in advance was stated for all 36 definitions, and it was also met
there (S1).

A reader who took one definition would report a small negative relation (the stall baseline, any fixed stall
threshold, a 10-s pause threshold), a negligible one (most pause thresholds at 2 or 5~s), or a positive one (a 15-s
stall window with a writer quantile); a writer-quantile pause threshold would report the rate of key presses.

\subsection{Adjusting for essay length leaves no stall definition negative}
\label{sec:res-adjust}

Two quantities that do not contain the labels can carry the association: how much a writer writes and how fast.
Essay length correlated with the score at 0.75 [0.73, 0.76], and faster writers had higher scores (Spearman $-0.53$
between the score and the median short interval, a lower interval meaning faster typing). The length of observation
barely varied in KLiCKe (interquartile range 26--29~min) and correlated with the score at 0.10.

\paragraph{Given essay length.} Given essay length and the length of observation, the partial correlation of score and
stall rate was positive with an interval above zero for 54 definitions, included zero for 35 and was below zero for
none ($+0.02$ to $+0.13$; baseline $+0.057$ [$+0.029$, $+0.085$]); given essay length alone, 59 were positive and none
negative. The 10 definitions that were positive without adjustment stayed positive. By the reading set before the
computation, the direction became uniform: no definition was negative, although 35 included zero. Stall time and essay
length overlap by construction, since time in a stall is time in which no text is added, so the adjustment also removes
part of what the stall rate measures. For pauses, 33 of 36 were positive and none negative.

\paragraph{Given typing speed (exploratory).} The speed covariate was chosen after seeing results
(Section~\ref{sec:status}). With the median of intervals under 2~s, the partial correlation of score and stall rate was
negative with an interval below zero for all 89 definitions ($-0.29$ to $-0.03$; baseline $-0.18$ [$-0.21$, $-0.15$]).
Over six ways of measuring speed, 532 of 534 combinations of a definition and a measure had an interval below zero
and 2 included zero, which met the reading set in advance that the direction would ``also depend on how speed is
measured''; none was positive (S3).
For pauses, the speed-adjusted correlation was negative for 4 definitions and positive for 29 (with fixed thresholds, 4
and 11).

Either adjustment made the direction of the stall association uniform, and the direction depended on the covariate:
given length it was never negative, given speed it was negative for every definition. Neither value is a causal effect
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{The same diagnostic applied to other conclusions}
\label{sec:res-other}

The diagnostic applies to any conclusion drawn from the labels. We applied it to three more: a comparison of rates
between datasets, a count model within writers, and the decomposition of stall length.

\paragraph{Comparing rates between datasets.}
\label{sec:res-ratio}
Does a comparison of stall rates between datasets survive a change of definition? Under the baseline the rates were
15.1 (KLiCKe), 13.1 (CS1 2019), 8.8 (CS1 2021) and 7.1 (CoAuthor) per hour; across the 89 definitions, the CS1 2019
rate alone ranged from 4.4 to 46.0. We counted a ratio as reversed beyond sampling variation when its point value and
its interval lay on the other side of 1 from the baseline ratio (Figure~\ref{fig:ratio}). For the two CS1 editions,
which share the course and the recording and which we had committed to report first, 20 of 89 definitions (22\%)
reversed the ratio, below the 25\% we had set after seeing point values (Section~\ref{sec:status}); 18 of the 20 used a
fixed $\theta$. Of the other pairs, CS1 2019 against KLiCKe (baseline ratio 0.86) reversed for 66 definitions (36 with a
fixed $\theta$, 30 with a writer quantile), CS1 2021 against KLiCKe for 53 (36 and 17), CS1 2019 against CoAuthor for 6
(all writer quantile) and the two writing datasets for none; the baseline interval of CS1 2021 against CoAuthor
included 1. Every fixed-$\theta$ definition thus reversed both comparisons with KLiCKe. In the pause family, the two CS1
editions reversed for none of 36, whereas CS1 2019 against CoAuthor reversed for 32 (14 fixed, 18 quantile) and
CoAuthor against KLiCKe for 31 (13 and 18), so the direction of a ratio changed with the definition in the pause
family too, by a criterion set after seeing point values; pause rates in CoAuthor were 42 to 498 per hour. Restricted to the time
after each unit's 100th interval, the two CS1 editions reversed for 22 (19 with a fixed $\theta$), still below 25\%
(S2). The counts are not comparable across pairs, whose baseline ratios and sample sizes differ. The rank order of the
four datasets changed for 80 of 89 definitions, a weak result because the close baseline rates of CS1 2019 and KLiCKe
swap alone in 11 of them.

\paragraph{Departure from a constant rate within writers.}
\label{sec:res-phi}
Does the simplest count model, in which a writer stalls at a constant rate, fit the counts of a writer's sub-units? We
measured the dispersion index $\varphi$ with the writer's own rate as the expectation (Table~\ref{tab:phi},
Figure~\ref{fig:phi}). Under the baseline, $\varphi$ exceeded 1 in all four CS1 cells (1.44 to 3.82) and not in
CoAuthor sessions (1.08). Across the 89 definitions, the median $\varphi$ was close to 1 in CS1 2019 blocks and CoAuthor
sessions (1.03 and 1.02), 1.21 in CS1 2021 blocks and 1.25 and 2.28 in the CS1 assignments; the interval exceeded 1 for
43 to 68 definitions in the CS1 cells and for 7 in CoAuthor. The baseline lay above the median of the 89 definitions in
every CS1 cell. The conclusion against 1.5 reversed beyond sampling variation for 54 definitions in the CS1 2019
assignments, 49 in the CS1 2021 blocks, 19 (21\%) in the CS1 2021 assignments and none in CoAuthor; in CS1 2019 blocks
the baseline interval contained 1.5, so no reversal was counted. Restricted to the time after the 100th interval, the
CS1 2021 assignments reversed for 26 (29\%), crossing 25\% (S2). The definitions that lowered $\varphi$ most split the
observation at key-press gaps of 5~min or used a fixed 30-s threshold (S4). Whether stall counts depart from a constant
rate within writers is, in these data, a property of the definition.

\paragraph{Components of stall length.}
\label{sec:res-length}
A map of what moves needs a side that stays. Is the decomposition of stall length as fragile as the rates? We fitted
the decomposition for all 90 definitions (Figure~\ref{fig:length}). Under the baseline, the stall-to-stall share was
0.93 (CS1 2019) and 0.92 (CS1 2021), and the writer share 0.03 in both, which can be read as the intraclass correlation
of writers. In both CS1 editions, the stall-to-stall share stayed the largest component in every one of the 89
definitions (0.88 to 1.00), at least 0.83 above the next component, and the writer share stayed small (0.00 to 0.05);
we did not compute intervals for these shares. The excluded unresumed stalls were 0.5--7\% of the CS1
stalls, depending on the definition. CoAuthor kept the same order in 86 of 89 definitions; in the three
with $W = 60$~s, a 0.995 quantile and the characters-only rule, only 86 to 88 stalls were detected and the session share
exceeded the stall share. This is a weak contrast: most variance sitting at the lowest level is common for event-level
outcomes (S5).
