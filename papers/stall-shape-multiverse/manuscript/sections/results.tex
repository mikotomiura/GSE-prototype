\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The strength of the stall--essay-score association moves from negligible to small-to-moderate across
definitions, and turns weakly positive in one corner}
\label{sec:res-score}

A study that reports how a keystroke stall rate relates to the quality of a product usually reports one definition. We
asked whether that association is robust to the choice of definition, and measured, for each of the 89 definitions,
the Spearman correlation between the KLiCKe writers' stall rates and their holistic scores, with a resampled interval
(Figure~\ref{fig:score}) [A].

Under the baseline definition the correlation was $-0.063$ [$-0.091$, $-0.034$], negligible by the convention of
Section~\ref{sec:measures}. Across the 89 definitions it ranged from $-0.25$ to $+0.14$ (median $-0.14$). The interval
excluded zero on the negative side for 72 definitions, on the positive side for 10, and included zero for 7. Fifty-eight
definitions gave a negative correlation of at least 0.1 in absolute value and 3 a positive one [D]. The direction split
within one construct: among the 53 definitions with a writer-quantile $\theta$, the interval was positive for 10,
negative for 36 and included zero for 7, whereas all 36 fixed-second definitions were negative [D]. The 10 positive
definitions all had the shortest window, $W = 15$~s, the level we set at half the default; among the 59 definitions
without it, none was positive. The positive corner is not a floor effect of rare stalls: the share of writers with no
stall was 0.1--1.3\% in these 10 definitions, against 0.1--35\% across all 89 [D]. Within each of the four prompts, the
median correlation was negative for 74 definitions and positive for 15 (point values; S3).

The association that a reader would take away from a single definition is therefore not a property of the data alone.
With the baseline, a reader would report a negligible negative relation; with a fixed 10-s or 30-s threshold, a small
negative one; with a 15-s window and a writer-quantile threshold, a weak positive one.

\paragraph{Adjusting for typing speed [E].} Faster writers had higher scores ($\rho = -0.53$ between score and the
median short interval). We stated before computing the six speed measures that we would read the result as ``the
direction of the speed-adjusted association also depends on how speed is measured''; that is what we found, although
no adjusted correlation was positive. With five of the six measures, the partial correlation of score and stall rate
given speed was negative with an interval excluding zero for all 89 definitions (for the median of intervals under
2~s, $-0.29$ to $-0.03$; baseline $-0.18$ [$-0.21$, $-0.15$]); with speed measured only on intervals between two
character presses, 87 were negative and 2 included zero. Within prompts the result was the same, 89 of 89 negative
(Figure~\ref{fig:score}, lower panel; S3). The stall rate correlated with speed mainly in the writer-quantile family
(median $-0.12$, against $+0.02$ for fixed seconds), where faster writers had more stalls; this is the pattern of a
suppression \cite{mackinnon2000equivalence}, which we did not test (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{The rank and ratio of rates between datasets move with the definition, and the CS1--essay ratios reverse}
\label{sec:res-ratio}

Comparisons between datasets or populations rest on the rate of stalls. We asked whether the order and the ratios of
the four datasets' rates survive a change of definition. Under the baseline the rates were 15.1 (KLiCKe), 13.1 (CS1
2019), 8.8 (CS1 2021) and 7.1 (CoAuthor) stalls per hour; across the 89 definitions, the CS1 2019 rate alone ranged from
4.4 to 46.0. The rank order of the four datasets differed from the baseline order for 80 of 89 definitions [P], and for
44 of the 53 writer-quantile definitions [D].

We counted a ratio as reversed beyond sampling variation when its interval lay entirely on the other side of 1 from
the baseline ratio (Figure~\ref{fig:ratio}) [A]. For CS1 2019 against KLiCKe, whose rates are close under the baseline
(ratio 0.86), 66 of 89 definitions reversed the ratio, and the log ratio ranged from $-0.75$ to $+0.90$. CS1 2021 against
KLiCKe reversed for 53, CS1 2019 against CoAuthor for 6, and the two writing datasets for none; the baseline interval of
CS1 2021 against CoAuthor included 1, so that pair has no baseline direction. For the two CS1 editions, which share the
course and the recording, 20 of 89 definitions (22\%) reversed the ratio, below the 25\% we had set; 18 of the 20 used a
fixed $\theta$, and only 2 of the 53 writer-quantile definitions reversed it. The counts are not comparable across pairs,
whose baseline ratios and sample sizes differ, and the differences between datasets are not attributed to a cause
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

A comparison of stall rates between a programming course and an essay corpus can thus come out either way depending on
the definition, while a comparison between two editions of the same course is mostly stable unless the threshold
family changes.

\subsection{Whether stall counts are overdispersed within writers depends on the definition}
\label{sec:res-phi}

A count model for stalls per unit assumes, at its simplest, that a writer stalls at a constant rate. We asked whether
the stall counts of a writer's sub-units vary more than that, and measured the dispersion index $\varphi$ with the
writer's own rate as the expectation (Table~\ref{tab:phi}, Figure~\ref{fig:phi}) [P, A].

Under the baseline, $\varphi$ exceeded 1 in all four CS1 cells (1.44 to 3.82) and not in CoAuthor sessions (1.08).
Across the 89 definitions, the median $\varphi$ was close to 1 in three of the five cells (1.03, 1.21 and 1.02) and above
it in the CS1 assignments (1.25 and 2.28); the interval exceeded 1 for 43 to 68 definitions in the CS1 cells and for 7 in
CoAuthor. The baseline lay above the median of the 89 definitions in every CS1 cell. The conclusion against 1.5 reversed
beyond sampling variation for 54 definitions in the CS1 2019 assignments and 49 in the CS1 2021 blocks. The definitions
that lowered $\varphi$ most split the observation at key-press gaps of 5~min or used a fixed 30-s threshold (S4).

Whether a negative binomial model is needed for stall counts within a writer is, in these data, a property of the
definition as much as of the writers.

\subsection{In CS1, the largest component of the variation in stall length does not depend on the definition}
\label{sec:res-length}

A map of what moves needs a side that stays. We asked whether the decomposition of stall length is as fragile as the
rates, and fitted the decomposition for all 90 definitions (Figure~\ref{fig:length}) [A]. Under the baseline, the
stall-to-stall share was 0.93 (CS1 2019) and 0.92 (CS1 2021), and the writer share 0.03 in both, which can be read as
the intraclass correlation of writers. In both CS1 editions the stall-to-stall share stayed the largest component in
every one of the 89 definitions (0.88 to 1.00), and the writer share stayed small (0.00 to 0.05). CoAuthor kept the same
order in 86 of 89 definitions; in the three with $W = 60$~s, a 0.995 quantile and the characters-only rule, only 86 to
88 stalls were detected and the session share exceeded the stall share. That most of the variance sits at the lowest
level is a common pattern for event-level outcomes; the result is that neither its size nor the smallness of the
writer share moved with the definition.
