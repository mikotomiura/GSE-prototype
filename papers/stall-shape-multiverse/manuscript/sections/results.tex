\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The direction of the label--essay-score association changes within each detector}
\label{sec:res-score}

A study that reports how a pause or stall rate relates to the quality of a product usually reports one definition. We
asked whether that association depends on the definition, and measured, for each definition, the Spearman
correlation between the KLiCKe writers' rates and their holistic scores, with a resampled interval
(Figure~\ref{fig:score}, Table~\ref{tab:pause}).

\paragraph{Stalls.} Under the baseline the correlation was $-0.063$ [$-0.073$, $-0.019$]. Two criteria had been set
before the association was seen. The presence of an association would count as moving if the share of definitions
with an interval excluding zero lay between 25\% and 75\%; it was 82 of 89 (92\%), so by that criterion presence did not
move. The direction would count as changing if one definition had an interval on the other side of zero; 14 did, so the
direction changed. All 14 had the shortest window, $W = 15$~s, the level we set at half the default and for which we
found no precedent, and a writer-quantile $\theta$; among the 59 definitions without it, 53 were negative and none was
positive. Across the 89 definitions the correlation ranged from $-0.25$ to $+0.14$ (median $-0.14$), with intervals
negative for 68, positive for 14 and including zero for 7. In strength, 58 definitions gave a negative correlation of
at least 0.1 in absolute value and 3 a positive one; all 36 fixed-second definitions were negative. The positive corner
is not a floor effect of rare stalls: the share of writers with no stall was 0.1--1.3\% there, against 0.1--35\%
across all 89. These are pooled over the four prompts; within prompts, as point values only, the median correlation was
negative for 74 definitions and positive for 15 (S3).

\paragraph{Pauses.} The second detector gave the same kind of result with levels in prior use. With fixed thresholds,
the correlation was negative with an interval below zero for all six definitions at 10~s ($-0.06$ to $-0.13$), around
zero at 5~s, and positive with an interval above zero for two of the three definitions on character intervals at 2~s
($+0.045$ and $+0.049$) and one at 5~s. With a writer quantile it was positive for all 18 definitions ($+0.58$ to
$+0.67$): a quantile threshold counts a fixed share of each writer's intervals, so its rate follows the writer's rate of
key presses. Over the 36 definitions, intervals were negative for 7, positive for 21 and included zero for 8, so by the
same criterion the direction changed.

A reader who took one definition would report a negligible or small negative relation (the stall baseline, any fixed
stall threshold, a 10-s pause threshold), no relation (a 5-s pause threshold), or a positive one (a 15-s stall window
with a writer quantile, a 2-s pause threshold on character intervals, or any writer-quantile pause threshold).

\subsection{Adjustment moves the association as much as the definition}
\label{sec:res-adjust}

Two quantities that do not contain the labels can carry the association: how fast a writer types and how much a
writer writes. Faster writers had higher scores (Spearman $-0.53$ between the score and the median short interval, a
lower interval meaning faster typing), and essay length correlated with the score at 0.75 [0.73, 0.76]. The length of
observation barely varied in KLiCKe (interquartile range 26--29~min) and correlated with the score at 0.10.

\paragraph{Given typing speed.} This adjustment is exploratory (Section~\ref{sec:status}). With the median of intervals
under 2~s, the partial correlation of score and stall rate was negative with an interval below zero for all 89
definitions ($-0.29$ to $-0.03$; baseline $-0.18$ [$-0.21$, $-0.15$]). Over the six ways of measuring speed, 532 of the
534 combinations of a definition and a measure had an interval below zero and 2 included zero (speed on intervals
between character presses only); none was positive, and within prompts the result was 89 of 89 negative (S3). The
reading stated in advance, that the direction would ``also depend on how speed is measured'' if any interval included
zero or was positive, was triggered by those 2 combinations; no direction changed.

\paragraph{Given essay length.} Given essay length and the length of observation, the partial correlation of score and
stall rate was positive with an interval above zero for 54 definitions, included zero for 35 and was below zero for
none ($+0.02$ to $+0.13$; baseline $+0.057$ [$+0.029$, $+0.085$]); given essay length alone, 59 were positive and none
negative. The 14 definitions that were positive without adjustment stayed positive. For pauses, 33 of 36 were positive
and none negative. By the reading set before the computation, adjusting for length makes the direction uniform, and
positive.

The two adjustments move the association in opposite directions: given speed it is negative for every definition, given
length it is never negative. Neither value is a causal effect (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{The rank and ratio of rates between datasets move with the definition}
\label{sec:res-ratio}

Comparisons between datasets or populations rest on the rate. We asked whether the order and the ratios of the four
datasets' rates survive a change of definition. Under the baseline the stall rates were 15.1 (KLiCKe), 13.1 (CS1
2019), 8.8 (CS1 2021) and 7.1 (CoAuthor) per hour; across the 89 definitions, the CS1 2019 rate alone ranged from 4.4 to
46.0. The rank order of the four datasets differed from the baseline order for 80 of 89 definitions; this is a weak
result, because the baseline rates of CS1 2019 and KLiCKe are close and their swap alone accounts for 11 of the 80.

We counted a ratio as reversed beyond sampling variation when its interval lay entirely on the other side of 1 from
the baseline ratio (Figure~\ref{fig:ratio}). For the two CS1 editions, which share the course and the recording and
which we had committed to report first, 20 of 89 definitions (22\%) reversed the ratio, below the 25\% we had set; 18 of
the 20 used a fixed $\theta$. Among the other pairs, CS1 2019 against KLiCKe (baseline ratio 0.86) reversed for 64
definitions, with log ratios from $-0.75$ to $+0.90$; CS1 2021 against KLiCKe for 52; CS1 2019 against CoAuthor for 9;
and the two writing datasets for none (the baseline interval of CS1 2021 against CoAuthor included 1). In the pause
family, the two CS1 editions reversed for none of 36, whereas CS1 2019 against CoAuthor reversed for 32 and CoAuthor
against KLiCKe for 31; pause rates in CoAuthor were 42 to 498 per hour. Restricted to the time after each unit's 100th interval, where both
threshold families can raise a stall, the two CS1 editions reversed for 22 definitions (19 with a fixed $\theta$), still below
25\%, and the other pairs for 66, 53, 8 and 2 (S2). The counts are not comparable
across pairs, whose baseline ratios and sample sizes differ.

\subsection{Whether stall counts depart from a constant rate within writers depends on the definition}
\label{sec:res-phi}

The simplest count model for stalls per unit assumes that a writer stalls at a constant rate. We asked whether the
stall counts of a writer's sub-units vary more than that, and measured the dispersion index $\varphi$ with the writer's
own rate as the expectation (Table~\ref{tab:phi}, Figure~\ref{fig:phi}).

Under the baseline, $\varphi$ exceeded 1 in all four CS1 cells (1.44 to 3.82) and not in CoAuthor sessions (1.08).
Across the 89 definitions, the median $\varphi$ was close to 1 in CS1 2019 blocks and CoAuthor sessions (1.03 and 1.02),
1.21 in CS1 2021 blocks and 1.25 and 2.28 in the CS1 assignments; the interval exceeded 1 for 43 to 61 definitions in the CS1 cells and for 12
in CoAuthor. The baseline lay above the median of the 89 definitions in every CS1 cell. The conclusion against 1.5
reversed beyond sampling variation for 55 definitions in the CS1 2019 assignments and 54 in the CS1 2021 blocks;
restricted to the time after the 100th interval, also for 26 in the CS1 2021 assignments (S2). The
definitions that lowered $\varphi$ most split the observation at key-press gaps of 5~min or used a fixed 30-s threshold
(S4). Whether stall counts depart from a constant rate within writers is, in these data, a property of the definition.

\subsection{In CS1, the largest component of the variation in stall length stays the same}
\label{sec:res-length}

A map of what moves needs a side that stays. We asked whether the decomposition of stall length is as fragile as the
rates, and fitted the decomposition for all 90 definitions (Figure~\ref{fig:length}). Under the baseline, the
stall-to-stall share was 0.93 (CS1 2019) and 0.92 (CS1 2021), and the writer share 0.03 in both, which can be read as
the intraclass correlation of writers. In both CS1 editions, the stall-to-stall share stayed the largest component in
every one of the 89 definitions (0.88 to 1.00), and the writer share stayed small (0.00 to 0.05). CoAuthor kept the same
order in 86 of 89 definitions; in the three with $W = 60$~s, a 0.995 quantile and the characters-only rule, only 86 to
88 stalls were detected and the session share exceeded the stall share. This is a weak contrast: most variance sitting
at the lowest level is common for event-level outcomes (S5).
